### 5.2. Harms of Cultural Homogenization

Neocolonialism refers to the practice of using economic, political, cultural, or technological forces by Western nations to indirectly control formerly colonized nations. Unlike traditional colonialism, which involved direct territorial control, neocolonialism operates through more subtle mechanisms, such as control over resources, markets, institutions, or the spread of dominant cultural and ideological values ([Scott and Marshall, 2009]; [Halperin, 2024]). In recent years, a new form of neo-colonialism has emerged, mediated by advances in big data and AI, where the digital realm is used to move wealth from the poor to the rich, a dynamic reminiscent of colonialism. ([Couldry and Mejias, 2019a]). A growing body of scholarly work points to the harms of this _data colonization_ ([Couldry and Mejias, 2019b]) and _AI colonialism_ ([Tacheva and Ramasubramanian, 2023]; [Hao, 2022]) and argues how everyday products and services offered by big tech companies based in the West capitalize on and profit from the data extracted from people in non-Western settings to enrich the wealthy and powerful. An example of this form of colonialism is how billion-dollar tech companies such as Amazon, Facebook, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI paying pennies to workers in developing nations to label training data ([Rowe, 2023]), only to build products that center Western norms ([Shahid and Vashistha, 2023]). In this neocolonialism, these companies establish Western influence not by political or military control, but by consolidating Western power over data, cultural expression, and information flows, inviting calls from critical scholars to decolonize computing ([Irani et al., 2010]; [Ali, 2016]; [Mohamed et al., 2020]).

Our study provides concrete evidence of AI colonialism that is more worrying than an AI model’s internal biases: such models alter cultural preferences and descriptions of cultural artifacts even under human oversight. This homogenization reflects a form of cultural imperialism ([Landsman, 2014]), where one culture dominates and suppresses the plurality of knowledge, practices, and languages, reinforcing Western hegemony over values.

In addition to explicit cultural imperialism, the homogenization effect can also lead to an implicit cognitive imperialism ([Battiste and Henderson, 2000]). Continuously subjecting users to mono-cultural suggestions portrays Western norms and values as the only truth, reinforcing the supremacy of “only one language, one culture, one frame of reference” ([Battiste and Henderson, 2000]). This cultural hegemony may encourage users to think, write, and reason in ways that reflect Western values.

### 5.2. Harms of Cultural Homogenization

Neocolonialism refers to the practice of using economic, political, cultural, or technological forces by Western nations to indirectly control formerly colonized nations. Unlike traditional colonialism, which involved direct territorial control, neocolonialism operates through more subtle mechanisms, such as control over resources, markets, institutions, or the spread of dominant cultural and ideological values ([Scott and Marshall, 2009](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib76); [Halperin, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib34)). In recent years, a new form of neo-colonialism has emerged, mediated by advances in big data and AI, where the digital realm is used to move wealth from the poor to the rich, a dynamic reminiscent of colonialism. ([Couldry and Mejias, 2019a](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib20)). A growing body of scholarly work points to the harms of this _data colonization_ ([Couldry and Mejias, 2019b](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib21)) and _AI colonialism_ ([Tacheva and Ramasubramanian, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib82); [Hao, 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib35)) and argues how everyday products and services offered by big tech companies based in the West capitalize on and profit from the data extracted from people in non-Western settings to enrich the wealthy and powerful. An example of this form of colonialism is how billion-dollar tech companies such as Amazon, Facebook, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI paying pennies to workers in developing nations to label training data ([Rowe, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib73)), only to build products that center Western norms ([Shahid and Vashistha, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib77)). In this neocolonialism, these companies establish Western influence not by political or military control, but by consolidating Western power over data, cultural expression, and information flows, inviting calls from critical scholars to decolonize computing ([Irani et al., 2010](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib43); [Ali, 2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib4); [Mohamed et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib61)).

Our study provides concrete evidence of AI colonialism that is more worrying than an AI model’s internal biases: such models alter cultural preferences and descriptions of cultural artifacts even under human oversight. This homogenization reflects a form of cultural imperialism ([Landsman, 2014](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib49)), where one culture dominates and suppresses the plurality of knowledge, practices, and languages, reinforcing Western hegemony over values. For example, the growing use of AI in story-writing interfaces ([Singh et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib80); [Lee et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib51); [Yang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib88); [Yuan et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib89)) can gradually erode traditional storytelling methods and creative expressions. Similarly, Western-biased AI suggestions in email clients may encourage a direct and informal tone, which could lead to conflicts with superiors in cultures that observe an established social hierarchy ([Hofstede, 2011](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib39)) and expect formal language as a sign of respect. More broadly, cultures differ in directness, formality, and politeness ([Hershcovich et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib37)), distinct stylistic variations that are at risk of being lost to this homogenization. Our work highlights one such change (lexical diversity) and lays the groundwork for further research to identify more of these subtle shifts. This will require interdisciplinary effort among linguists, critical scholars, and AI researchers.

In addition to explicit cultural imperialism, the homogenization effect can also lead to an implicit cognitive imperialism ([Battiste and Henderson, 2000](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib11)). Writing is closely tied to thinking, and what one writes can transform what one thinks ([Menary, 2007](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib58)). Continuously subjecting users to mono-cultural suggestions portrays Western norms and values as the only truth, reinforcing the supremacy of “only one language, one culture, one frame of reference” ([Battiste and Henderson, 2000](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib11)). This cultural hegemony may encourage users to think, write, and reason in ways that reflect Western values. Over time, this can affect how individuals perceive their own culture, potentially leading to feelings of cultural inferiority or a loss of cultural identity.
