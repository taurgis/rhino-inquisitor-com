### 1.1 Broader context of the study

The previous studies on the stylometry of LLMs have primarily focused on developing automatic human / AI classifiers, or AI detection systems. Recent studies have demonstrated various approaches to distinguishing AI-generated content, with accuracy rates ranging from 81% to 98% using stylometric features ([Kumarage et al., 2023a]; [Mikros et al., 2023a]; [Mikros, 2025a]; [Przystalski et al., 2026a]).

The current consensus appears to be that there is no single distinct AI language, e.g., ChatGPT and Gemini default personas exhibit their own unique writing styles, akin to human idiolects (let us coin the term _aidiolects_ for AI idiolects) ([Rudnicka, 2025c]; [Bitton et al., 2025a]; [Rudnicka, 2025b]; [Rao et al., 2025a]).

Nevertheless, we can identify certain stylistic attractors — patterns that all or at least most LLMs tend to gravitate toward. Furthermore, it is clear that some personas cannot be simulated by LLM-based systems because they are simply missing from their latent space — either because they are not represented in training data (such as an illiterate person, see [Silva et al., 2024a]) or because they are deliberately suppressed in subsequent post-training (because of political extremism or political views inconsistent with the values of the companies that train these models, see [Rozado, 2024a]).

How should one characterize these underlying stylistic biases or attractors? In the tradition of Biber’s Multi-Dimensional Analysis (MDA) [Biber, 1988a], we consider that co-occurring linguistic features often reflect an underlying stylistic dimension. If an LLM-generated text contains less past-tense verbs and far less third-person pronouns than a human reference, it’s likely not a coincidence — those features are both indicators that the model’s style is shifted towards less narrativity. These two features (among many others) co-occur commonly in fiction and storytelling genres (which often involve past events and third-person characters) and are jointly absent in, let us say, legal contracts, so they formed a _Narrativity_ dimension in Biber’s factor analysis.

Crucially, these dimensions are interpretable: instead of saying a text has 50% more second person pronouns, 30% extra present tense verbs, disproportionate number of “private” verbs ( _assume, believe_…), and shorter words on average (which is hard to contextualize), we can say it scores high on the Involved dimension (pointing to an informal, interactive style), for example.

## Detailed

### 1.1 Broader context of the study

The previous studies on the stylometry of LLMs have primarily focused on developing automatic human / AI classifiers, or AI detection systems. Recent studies have demonstrated various approaches to distinguishing AI-generated content, with accuracy rates ranging from 81% to 98% using stylometric features ([Kumarage et al., 2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx16); [Mikros et al., 2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx19); [Mikros, 2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx18); [Przystalski et al., 2026a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx27)). However, our study approaches stylometry in different aims in mind. While AI detection systems examine differences in style in order to exploit them, we look at them to evaluate and interpret the quality of the LLM.

The current consensus appears to be that there is no single distinct AI language, e.g., ChatGPT and Gemini default personas exhibit their own unique writing styles, akin to human idiolects (let us coin the term _aidiolects_ for AI idiolects) ([Rudnicka, 2025c](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx33); [Bitton et al., 2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx7); [Rudnicka, 2025b](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx32); [Rao et al., 2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx28)). At the same time, LLMs can simulate many different personas than the default ones ([Shanahan et al., 2023a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx35); [Milička et al., 2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx23)) and these personas produce stylistically diverse texts ([Malik et al., 2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx17)).

Nevertheless, we can identify certain stylistic attractors — patterns that all or at least most LLMs tend to gravitate toward. This is what actually makes all these human / AI classifiers mentioned in the first paragraph of this section possible. These attractors are driven by similar training data largely derived from Common Crawl ([Common Crawl Foundation, 2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx9)) and other easy to get sources, and of course by the architecture itself ([Vaswani et al., 2017a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx36)). Furthermore, it is clear that some personas cannot be simulated by LLM-based systems because they are simply missing from their latent space — either because they are not represented in training data (such as an illiterate person, see [Silva et al., 2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx12)) or because they are deliberately suppressed in subsequent post-training (because of political extremism or political views inconsistent with the values of the companies that train these models, see [Rozado, 2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx31)).

How should one characterize these underlying stylistic biases or attractors? Rather than cataloging individual quirks (like overusing certain words or constructions or other stylometric features, like in [Reinhart et al., 2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx29)), it is useful to adopt a holistic, corpus-linguistic approach. In the tradition of Biber’s Multi-Dimensional Analysis (MDA) [Biber, 1988a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx5), we consider that co-occurring linguistic features often reflect an underlying stylistic dimension. If an LLM-generated text contains less past-tense verbs and far less third-person pronouns than a human reference, it’s likely not a coincidence — those features are both indicators that the model’s style is shifted towards less narrativity. These two features (among many others) co-occur commonly in fiction and storytelling genres (which often involve past events and third-person characters) and are jointly absent in, let us say, legal contracts, so they formed a _Narrativity_ dimension in Biber’s factor analysis.

Crucially, these dimensions are interpretable: instead of saying a text has 50% more second person pronouns, 30% extra present tense verbs, disproportionate number of “private” verbs ( _assume, believe_…), and shorter words on average (which is hard to contextualize), we can say it scores high on the Involved dimension (pointing to an informal, interactive style), for example. Such dimensions have been corroborated by follow-up studies in corpus linguistics and shown to align with our intuitive perceptions of genre differences [Biber, 2019a](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179#biba.bibx6).
