### 2.2. AI-Based Writing Support

Advances in computing have been extensively used to provide writing support, starting with tools like spell-check ([Peterson, 1980]) and grammar-check ([Leacock et al., 2010]) to improve writing efficiency ([Buschek et al., 2021]). As natural language generation technologies evolved, researchers began applying them to support creative writing pursuits such as story writing ([Dhillon et al., 2024]). Some examples include providing next-word or next-sentence suggestions ([Clark et al., 2018]; [Manjavacas et al., 2017]; [Roemmele and Gordon, 2018]), and generating creative content (e.g., poems) based on a given topic or writing style ([Ghazvininejad et al., 2016]; [Gabriel et al., 2015]). However, early AI models struggled to capture the intent of the writer, rendering these tools as passive ideation tools rather than active writing assistants ([Gero and Chilton, 2019b]) (e.g., metaphor generation ([Gero and Chilton, 2019a]; [Chakrabarty et al., 2021])).

However, recent advancements in natural language generation, driven by LLMs, have led to a resurgence of tools to support open-ended writing ([Dhillon et al., 2024]), such as story generation ([Singh et al., 2023]; [Lee et al., 2022]; [Yang et al., 2022]; [Yuan et al., 2022]) and screenplay writing ([Mirowski et al., 2023]). Beyond creative writing, HCI researchers have also proposed tools for argumentative writing, including journalism ([Petridis et al., 2023]), scientific writing ([Gero et al., 2021]), email writing ([Kannan et al., 2016]), and crafting self-introductions for networking ([Hui et al., 2018]). These tools are designed to work in situ, as active collaborators, offering writing suggestions through pop-ups, side panels, or inline predictions ([Chen et al., 2019]).

With the popularity of such tools, researchers have also examined the broader implications of AI-powered writing suggestions, including their effect on users and the resulting text ([Buschek et al., 2021]). As a result, studies have shown mixed results regarding the efficiency benefits of writing suggestions ([Dhillon et al., 2024]; [Quinn and Zhai, 2016]; [Bhat et al., 2023]), indicating that such gains may be context-dependent.

Furthermore, there has been growing interest in the risks of writing support tools, especially as they are powered by language models that reflect social biases. We extend this line of work by presenting the first cross-cultural study of writing suggestions, specifically designed to measure the cultural harms that may arise from these tools. In contrast, we present a systematic study that explicitly investigates cross-cultural differences in the context of writing suggestions from culturally biased models.

### 2.2. AI-Based Writing Support

Advances in computing have been extensively used to provide writing support, starting with tools like spell-check ([Peterson, 1980](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib65)) and grammar-check ([Leacock et al., 2010](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib50)) to improve writing efficiency ([Buschek et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib14)). As natural language generation technologies evolved, researchers began applying them to support creative writing pursuits such as story writing ([Dhillon et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib22)). Some examples include providing next-word or next-sentence suggestions ([Clark et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib19); [Manjavacas et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib56); [Roemmele and Gordon, 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib72)), and generating creative content (e.g., poems) based on a given topic or writing style ([Ghazvininejad et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib32); [Gabriel et al., 2015](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib27)). However, early AI models struggled to capture the intent of the writer, rendering these tools as passive ideation tools rather than active writing assistants ([Gero and Chilton, 2019b](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib30)) (e.g., metaphor generation ([Gero and Chilton, 2019a](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib29); [Chakrabarty et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib16))).

However, recent advancements in natural language generation, driven by LLMs, have led to a resurgence of tools to support open-ended writing ([Dhillon et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib22)), such as story generation ([Singh et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib80); [Lee et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib51); [Yang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib88); [Yuan et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib89)) and screenplay writing ([Mirowski et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib59)). Beyond creative writing, HCI researchers have also proposed tools for argumentative writing, including journalism ([Petridis et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib66)), scientific writing ([Gero et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib31)), email writing ([Kannan et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib47)), and crafting self-introductions for networking ([Hui et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib42)). These tools are designed to work in situ, as active collaborators, offering writing suggestions through pop-ups, side panels, or inline predictions ([Chen et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib17)).

With the popularity of such tools, researchers have also examined the broader implications of AI-powered writing suggestions, including their effect on users and the resulting text ([Buschek et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib14)). For instance, [Arnold et al. (2016)](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib6) identified a trade-off between efficiency and ideation: while short single-word suggestions enhance efficiency without inspiring creativity, long multi-word suggestions offer inspiration but can be distracting. As a result, studies have shown mixed results regarding the efficiency benefits of writing suggestions ([Dhillon et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib22); [Quinn and Zhai, 2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib70); [Bhat et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib12)), indicating that such gains may be context-dependent. Despite the potential to improve quality and productivity, suggestions often lead to reduced user satisfaction and a loss of ownership over the text produced ([Dhillon et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib22); [Kadoma et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib46)).

Furthermore, there has been growing interest in the risks of writing support tools, especially as they are powered by language models that reflect social biases. For instance, [Jakesch et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib44) showed that co-writing with opinionated language models shifts users’ views to align with the model’s biases. [Kadoma et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib46) highlighted the differential impact of writing assistants on users from minoritized genders. We extend this line of work by presenting the first cross-cultural study of writing suggestions, specifically designed to measure the cultural harms that may arise from these tools. The most closely related work in this space is [Buschek et al. (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib14), who investigated the differential impact of multi-word suggestions on native and non-native English speakers. However, their study focused on a coarse-grained comparison of English language proficiency without controlling for cultural differences. In contrast, we present a systematic study that explicitly investigates cross-cultural differences in the context of writing suggestions from culturally biased models.
