## Detailed

### 1.3 Autistic Experiences with AI-Detection

Kling \[[23](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14729v1#bib.bib25)\] reported on a university professor’s experience of being falsely accused of using AI to write their emails. The professor believed the accusation could be attributed to being autistic and mentioned that receiving such allegations is a common experience for autistic people \[[23](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14729v1#bib.bib25)\]. An autistic student falsely accused by her professor had similar observations, citing the "formulaic" nature of her own writing and its potential similarity to AI \[[9](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14729v1#bib.bib37)\]. Gegg-Harrison and Quarterman \[[14](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14729v1#bib.bib39)\] used a small corpus of their own writing to test the false positive rates of several different popular AI-detection tools and saw much higher false positive rates than those reported by the makers of the tools themselves. They go on to discuss their own neurodivergence as a potential factor in these results, noting that a number of neurodivergent students and writers with similar suspicions reached out to them to share stories and fears related to false accusations of AI usage in their writing.
