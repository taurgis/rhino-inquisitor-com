Recent approaches attempting to quantify the increasing use of LLMs in scientific papers all build on the idea that LLM-written text differs from human-written text ([Lazebnik and Rosenfeld, 2024]; [Desaire et al., 2023]) and fall in three groups. One group of studies employed LLM detectors ([Tang et al., 2024b]), which are blackbox models trained to detect LLM writing based on ground-truth human and LLM texts ([Akram, 2024]; [Cheng et al., 2024]; [Liu and Bu, 2024]; [Picazo-Sanchez and Ortiz-Martin, 2024]). Another group of works explicitly modeled word frequency distribution in scientific corpora as a mixture distribution of texts produced by humans and by LLMs, again estimated using ground-truth human and LLM texts ([Liang et al., 2024b]; [Liang et al., 2024a]; [Geng and Trotta, 2024]; [Astarita et al., 2024]). The third group of studies relied on lists of marker words, known to be over-used by LLMs, which are typically stylistic words unrelated to the text content ([Gray, 2024]; [Liu and Bu, 2024]; [Matsui, 2024]).

All of these approaches share one common limitation: they require a ground-truth training set of LLM- and human-written texts. This setup can introduce biases ([Tang et al., 2024b]), as it requires assumptions on which models scientists use for their LLM-assisted writing, and how exactly they prompt them. Most importantly, all existing work focuses on detecting LLM texts, and none has attempted to systematically compare or relate LLM-induced changes in scientific writing to previous shifts in scholarly texts. This begs the question if the nature and magnitude of the observed changes are comparable to changes that regularly occur due to changing fashions, rising research topics, and global events such as the Covid-19 pandemic — or if LLMs impact scientific writing in an unprecedented way.

Here, we suggest a novel, data-driven approach to track LLM usage without requiring a labeled corpus of human-written versus model-generated texts. We adapt this idea to LLM-induced changes in word usage and track the excess use of words after the release of ChatGPT-like LLMs compared to pre-LLM years. Applying this analysis to the corpus of over 15 million 2010–2024 biomedical abstracts from the PubMed library allowed us to track changes in scientific writing over the last decade in the large field of biomedical research.

## 1 Introduction

When the world changes, human-written text changes. Major events like wars and revolutions affect word frequency distributions in text corpora ([Bochkarev et al., 2014](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib11)). The rise and fall of scientific disciplines is traceable in scholarly writing ([Hall et al., 2008](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib23); [Bizzoni et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib9)). Do technological advances leave a similar footprint on our writing?

With the release of ChatGPT in November 2022, human writing underwent an unprecedented change: For the first time, a large language model (LLM) was widely available that could generate and revise texts with human-like performance in several domains — including academia ([Ahmed et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib1)), where many have hoped that LLMs might lead to more equity ([Berdejo-Espinola and Amano, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib7)). Many researchers have since integrated LLMs in their daily writing tasks ([Van Noorden and Perkel, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib52)) and even co-authored papers with LLMs ([Stokel-Walker, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib47)). This has led to worries about research integrity, factual mistakes in LLM-generated content ([Mittelstadt et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib39); [Lindsay, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib35); [Walters and Wilder, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib54); [Ji et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib26); [Zhang et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib57)), and misuse of LLMs by so-called paper mills that produce fake publications ([Kendall and Teixeira da Silva, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib29)). These worries sparked attempts to track the footprint of LLM-assisted writing in scientific texts.

Recent approaches attempting to quantify the increasing use of LLMs in scientific papers all build on the idea that LLM-written text differs from human-written text ([Lazebnik and Rosenfeld, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib31); [Desaire et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib17)) and fall in three groups. One group of studies employed LLM detectors ([Tang et al., 2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib49)), which are blackbox models trained to detect LLM writing based on ground-truth human and LLM texts ([Akram, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib2); [Cheng et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib15); [Liu and Bu, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib36); [Picazo-Sanchez and Ortiz-Martin, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib45)). Another group of works explicitly modeled word frequency distribution in scientific corpora as a mixture distribution of texts produced by humans and by LLMs, again estimated using ground-truth human and LLM texts ([Liang et al., 2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib34); [Liang et al., 2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib33); [Geng and Trotta, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib19); [Astarita et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib4)). The third group of studies relied on lists of marker words, known to be over-used by LLMs, which are typically stylistic words unrelated to the text content ([Gray, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib21); [Liu and Bu, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib36); [Matsui, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib37)).

All of these approaches share one common limitation: they require a ground-truth training set of LLM- and human-written texts. Usually, human-written texts are obtained from pre-LLM years, while LLM-written texts are generated by a set of prompts. This setup can introduce biases ([Tang et al., 2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib49)), as it requires assumptions on which models scientists use for their LLM-assisted writing, and how exactly they prompt them. Furthermore, work based on LLM detector models suffers from their blackbox nature, as it does not allow further interrogation of the results on the word level, which can make their interpretation difficult. Most importantly, all existing work focuses on detecting LLM texts, and none has attempted to systematically compare or relate LLM-induced changes in scientific writing to previous shifts in scholarly texts. This begs the question if the nature and magnitude of the observed changes are comparable to changes that regularly occur due to changing fashions, rising research topics, and global events such as the Covid-19 pandemic — or if LLMs impact scientific writing in an unprecedented way.

Here, we suggest a novel, data-driven approach to track LLM usage without requiring a labeled corpus of human-written versus model-generated texts. We were inspired by studies of excess mortality ([Islam et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib25); [Karlinsky and Kobak, 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib28); [Msemburi et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib40)) that looked at the excess of fatalities during the Covid pandemic compared to pre-Covid mortality. We adapt this idea to LLM-induced changes in word usage and track the excess use of words after the release of ChatGPT-like LLMs compared to pre-LLM years. Applying this analysis to the corpus of over 15 million 2010–2024 biomedical abstracts from the PubMed library allowed us to track changes in scientific writing over the last decade in the large field of biomedical research. We found that the LLM-induced changes were unprecedented in both quality and quantity.
