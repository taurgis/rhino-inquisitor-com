[Padmakumar and He (2023)] and [Anderson et al. (2024a)] discuss how writing with LLMs unintentionally reduces content diversity, leading to homogenization. Current AI-assisted writing tools are powered by pre-trained language models that are refined through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) ([Ziegler et al., 2019]). RLHF transforms human preferences into training data to guide language models toward desired outcomes. The most common type of feedback used with RLHF is binary preferences between pairs of examples sampled from one or more language models ([Christiano et al., 2017]) (See Fig [1] Traditional Alignment). Editing undesirable portions of a response can be seen as an effective mechanism for enhancing alignment (See Fig [1] Alignment via Edits). The challenge, however, lies in consistently identifying and implementing edits that enhance quality while aligning with human preferences for effective writing. Prompting techniques that encourage the model to self-edit ([Madaan et al., 2024]) have shown promise; however, they do not work for long-form or paragraph-level writing ([Pan et al., 2024a]). The primary reason for this is that LLMs do not inherently know what aspects of the writing need improvement, how extensively they should edit, or how to make changes that align with any given writer’s expertise level and style.

To address these problems, we first create a comprehensive taxonomy of edit categories based on expert writing practices. We restrict our focus to generating text in literary fiction and creative non-fiction, as these genres challenge LLMs with their creativity, emotional nuance, and sophisticated language use. Finally, while expert writer’s edits are valuable for identifying idiosyncrasies, this approach may not scale for large text volumes. To address this, we design few-shot prompts that use writer’s edits to identify problematic spans in LLM-generated paragraphs and suggest improvements. Our work adds to the existing rich literature ([Liu et al., 2022]; [Jiang et al., 2022]; [Gao et al., 2024b]; [Yao et al., 2023]) on using completion edits to improve alignment in different tasks.

## 1\. Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) has the potential to revolutionize how we write, communicate, and express ideas ([Li et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib69)). Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of large language models (LLMs) in assisting with various writing tasks, including argumentative ([Lee et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib66); [Dhillon et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib25)), scientific ([Gero et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib44)), and creative writing ([Chakrabarty et al., 2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib17); [Ippolito et al., 2022b](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib52); [Yuan et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib118); [Mirowski et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib79); [Mirowski et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib78)). Aligning LLMs with human preferences ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib87)) has enabled their transformation into user-friendly tools for non-technical users, such as Google’s WorkSpace Labs, Grammarly, and Sudowrite. However, to truly benefit society, AI writing assistants must enhance human creativity and expression rather than homogenize content or diminish linguistic diversity ([Gabriel et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib40); [Jakesch et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib53))

This homogenization occurs not just at the semantic level but also at syntactic (structural), lexical ([Kobak et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib60)), and stylistic levels ([Shaib et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib104)). Additionally, LLM-generated texts are typically full of redundant exposition, overwrought metaphors, and florid descriptions due to verbosity bias during preference labeling ([Saito et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib102)).

The challenge, however, lies in consistently identifying and implementing edits that enhance quality while aligning with human preferences for effective writing. Prompting techniques that encourage the model to self-edit ([Madaan et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib73)) have shown promise; however, they do not work for long-form or paragraph-level writing ([Pan et al., 2024a](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib89)). To address these problems, we first create a comprehensive taxonomy of edit categories based on expert writing practices. We then recruit 18 writers to edit LLM-generated text using categories from our taxonomy. We define edits as changes that alter, replace, or refine specific phrases, clauses, or sentences within a larger text. We restrict our focus to generating text in literary fiction and creative non-fiction, as these genres challenge LLMs with their creativity, emotional nuance, and sophisticated language use. We focus on paragraph-level edits, as they balance granularity and scope, reducing costs and annotator fatigue. Paragraphs capture style and context better than sentences, enabling more cohesive improvements. Given LLMs’ limitations in long-term discourse coherence, paragraph-level enhancements facilitate human-AI collaboration. This approach allows humans to guide the overall structure and flow, while AI handles the lower-level details ([Dhillon et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib25)). Finally, while expert writer’s edits are valuable for identifying idiosyncrasies, this approach may not scale for large text volumes. To address this, we design few-shot prompts that use writer’s edits to identify problematic spans in LLM-generated paragraphs and suggest improvements. This method aims to enhance overall paragraph quality at scale. Our work adds to the existing rich literature ([Liu et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib71); [Jiang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib54); [Gao et al., 2024b](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib41); [Yao et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5#bib.bib117)) on using completion edits to improve alignment in different tasks.
