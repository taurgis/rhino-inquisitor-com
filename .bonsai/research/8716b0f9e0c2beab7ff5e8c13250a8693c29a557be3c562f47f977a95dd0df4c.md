##### No model surpasses 50% identification accuracy.

FLAWS is designed to be a challenging benchmark. As seen in Table [5], no model surpasses 50% identification accuracy even when considering the top 10 error candidates. In fact, the two worst performing models, Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro, have less than 30% identification accuracy at k\=10k=10. At k\=1k=1 values, where the least manual labor is needed, the identification accuracy is only 5-10% across all evaluated models, with GPT 5 and Grok 4 showing the strongest performance.

##### Comparison of the number of error text candidates identified.

At k\=10k=10, DeepSeek Reasoner v3.1 and GPT 5 achieve the strongest overall performance; however, their accuracy declines more sharply as kk decreases. For Gemini 2.5 Pro, accuracy stabilizes almost completely by k\=4k=4, while Grok 4 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 flatten around k\=6k=6. These differences align with the mean number of error text candidates that each model produces, shown in Table [8]. Although all models are prompted to identify and rank the top 10 most significant errors, they do not necessarily output 10 errors if they cannot detect that many. This is especially evident in the median counts: DeepSeek Reasoner v3.1 and GPT 5 typically produce 9 error spans, whereas Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Grok 4 produce around 5, and Gemini 2.5 Pro produces only 4. These observations have direct implications for how one should choose a model under different manual reviewing capacities. When sufficient manual review capacity is available, GPT 5 and DeepSeek Reasoner v3.1 may be preferable, as their performance scales with larger kk.

### 5.2 LLM Error Identification Results

We evaluated five state-of-the-art LLMs on the FLAWS benchmark. Our evaluation procedure aims to understand LLMs’ abilities to aid in human peer review. When deploying LLMs to support human reviewing workloads, it is important to display the most important errors first, since humans can feasibly review only a limited number of errors. As mentioned in Subsection [4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21843v1#S4.SS1 "4.1 Error Identification Task ‣ 4 Automated Evaluation Framework ‣ FLAWS: A Benchmark for Error Identificationand Localization in Scientific Papers"), we instruct models to output candidate errors in sorted order, and we report accuracy across different kk values, where k∈\[10\]k\\in\[10\]. One can treat kk as a proxy for manual effort, where a higher kk requires reviewing more candidates, increasing the manual workload.

Table [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21843v1#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5.2 LLM Error Identification Results ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ FLAWS: A Benchmark for Error Identificationand Localization in Scientific Papers") shows each model’s identification accuracy for its top-kk candidate error outputs. Over the whole benchmark, GPT 5 has the best performance, achieving an identification accuracy of 39.1% at k\=10k=10. However, while these raw accuracies provide an initial comparison, they do not account for the difference in sample sizes (448 Gemini 2.5 Pro\-inserted errors vs. 265 GPT 5\-inserted errors) and potential differences in the intrinsic difficulty of the inserted errors. In addition, this also does not account for potential bias in identification results if we use the same insertion and identification model.

In particular, the Bradley-Terry coefficient ([Bradley and Terry, 1952](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21843v1#bib.bib3)) — computed from a logistic regression model — is used to compare the performance and uncertainty estimates of pairwise evaluations in LLMs as well as other settings ([Chiang et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21843v1#bib.bib6); [Frick et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21843v1#bib.bib13)).
