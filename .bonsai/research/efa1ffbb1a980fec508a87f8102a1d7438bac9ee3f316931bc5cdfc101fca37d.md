## Abstract

### Background

Zipf's discovery that word frequency distributions obey a power law established parallels between biological and physical processes, and language, laying the groundwork for a complex systems perspective on human communication.

### Methodology/Principal Findings

By considering frequent words in USENET discussion groups and in disparate databases where the language has different levels of formality, here we show that the distributions of distances between successive occurrences of the same word display bursty deviations from a Poisson process and are well characterized by a stretched exponential (Weibull) scaling.

### Conclusions/Significance

Recurrence patterns of words are well described by a stretched exponential distribution of recurrence times, an empirical scaling that cannot be anticipated from Zipf's law.

## Introduction

Research on the distribution of time intervals between successive occurrences of events has revealed correspondences between natural phenomena on the one hand [\[1\]], [\[2\]] and social activities on the other hand [\[3\]]–[\[5\]].

The fields of statistical natural language processing and psycholinguistics study language from a dynamical point of view. Many widely used practical applications of statistical natural language processing, such as document retrieval based on keywords, also exploit dynamic patterns in word statistics [\[10\]], [\[14\]], [\[15\]]. Particularly important for these applications, and also noticed in different contexts [\[16\]]–[\[21\]], is the non-uniform distribution of content words through a text, suggesting that connections to the previous discoveries about inter-event distributions may be revealed through a systematic investigation of the recurrence times of different words.

With the rise of the Internet, large records of spontaneous and collective language are now available for scientific inquiry [\[22\]]–[\[24\]], allowing statistical questions about language to be investigated with an unprecedented precision.

Here, we show that long-time word recurrence patterns follow a stretched exponential distribution, owing to bursts and lulls in word usage. We focus on time scales that exceed the scale of _syntactic_ relations, and the burstiness of the words is driven by their semantics (that is, by what they mean). The burstiness of physical events and socially contextualized choices makes words more bursty than an exponential distribution. However, we show that words are typically less bursty than other human activities [\[26\]] due to their _logicality_ or _permutability_ [\[27\]], [\[28\]], technical constructs of formal semantics that index the extent to which the meanings and usage of words are stable over changes in the discourse context.

### Word Dependence

We have seen in [Fig. 1] that the word-dependent deviation from the exponential distribution is encapsulated in the parameter : the smaller the for any given word, the larger the deviation (see [Text S1], _Deviation from the Exponential Distribution_). Previous research has observed that frequent _function_ words (such as conjunctions and determiners) usually are closer to the random (Poisson) prediction while less frequent _content_ words (particularly names and common nouns) are more bursty. These observations were quantified using: (i) an entropic analysis of texts [\[16\]]; (ii) the variance of the sequence of recurrence times [\[17\]]; (iii) the recurrence time distribution [\[19\]], [\[42\]]; and (iv) the related distribution of the number of occurrences of words per document [\[14\]], [\[15\]]. Because we have a large database and do not bin the datastream into documents, we are able to go beyond these insightful works and systematically examine frequency and linguistic status as factors in word burstiness.

Our large database allows a detailed analysis of words that, despite being in the same frequency range, have very different statistical behavior. However, these examples also show that the distinction between function words and content words cannot be explanatory. Here we obtain a deeper level of explanation by drawing on tools from formal semantics, specifically on type theory [\[27\]], [\[43\]], [\[44\]], and on dynamic theories of semantics [\[45\]], [\[46\]], which model how words and sentences update the discourse context over time. We use semantics rather than syntax because syntax governs how words are combined into sentences, and we are interested in much longer time scales over which syntactic relations are not defined. Type theory establishes a scale from simple entities (e.g., proper nouns) to high type words (e.g., words that cannot be described using first-order logic, including intensional expressions and operators).

We are now in a position to discuss why burstiness depends on semantic Class. A more careful observer would connect the burstiness of words to the human decisions to perform activities related to the words. An insight on the problem of word usage can be obtained from Ref. [\[27\]], which establishes that the meaning and applicability of words with great _logicality_ remains invariant under _permutations_ of alternatives for the entities and relations specified in the constructions in which they appear. As a long discourse unfolds exploring different constructions, we expect words with higher permutability (higher semantic Class) to be more homogeneously distributed throughout the discourse and therefore have higher (be less bursty). However, the overt statistics of recurrence times do not need to be learned word by word. The fact that the behavior of almost all words deviate from a Poisson process to at least some extent, indicates that the permutability and usage of almost all words are contextually restricted to some degree, whether by their intrinsic meaning or by their social connotations.

### Conclusions

The quest for statistical laws in language has been driven both by applications in text mining and document retrieval, and by the desire for foundational understanding of humans as agents and participants in the world. Taking texts as examples of extended discourse, we combined these research agendas by showing that word meanings are directly related to their recurrence distributions via the permutability of concepts across discourse contexts. Our model for generating long-term recurrence patterns of words, a bag-of-words model with memory, is stationary and uniformly applicable to words of all parts of speech and semantic types. A word's position along the range in the memory parameter in the model, , effectively captures its position in between a power-law and an exponential distribution, thus capturing its degree of contextual anchoring.

Analogies between communicative dynamics and social dynamics more generally are suggested by the recent documentation of heavy-tailed distributions in many other human driven activities [\[3\]], [\[5\]], [\[26\]]. The stretched exponential form for recurrence distributions that derives from our model and the empirical finding it embodies are thus expected to also find applicability in other areas of human endeavor.

## Abstract

### Background

Zipf's discovery that word frequency distributions obey a power law established parallels between biological and physical processes, and language, laying the groundwork for a complex systems perspective on human communication. More recent research has also identified scaling regularities in the dynamics underlying the successive occurrences of events, suggesting the possibility of similar findings for language as well.

### Methodology/Principal Findings

By considering frequent words in USENET discussion groups and in disparate databases where the language has different levels of formality, here we show that the distributions of distances between successive occurrences of the same word display bursty deviations from a Poisson process and are well characterized by a stretched exponential (Weibull) scaling. The extent of this deviation depends strongly on semantic type – a measure of the logicality of each word – and less strongly on frequency. We develop a generative model of this behavior that fully determines the dynamics of word usage.

### Conclusions/Significance

Recurrence patterns of words are well described by a stretched exponential distribution of recurrence times, an empirical scaling that cannot be anticipated from Zipf's law. Because the use of words provides a uniquely precise and powerful lens on human thought and activity, our findings also have implications for other overt manifestations of collective human dynamics.

## Introduction

Research on the distribution of time intervals between successive occurrences of events has revealed correspondences between natural phenomena on the one hand [\[1\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Bak1), [\[2\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Bunde1) and social activities on the other hand [\[3\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Barabsi1)–[\[5\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Malmgren1). These studies consistently report bursty deviations both from random and from regular temporal distributions of events [\[6\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Goh1). Taken together, they suggest the existence of a dynamic counterpart to the universal scaling laws in magnitude and frequency distributions [\[7\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Zipf1)–[\[11\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Newman1). Language, understood as an embodied system of representation and communication [\[12\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Goodwin1), is a particularly interesting and promising domain for further exploration, because it both epitomizes social activity, and provides a medium for conceptualizing natural and biological reality.

The fields of statistical natural language processing and psycholinguistics study language from a dynamical point of view. Both treat language processing as encoding and decoding of information. In psycholinguistics, the local likelihood (or predictability) of words is a central focus of current research [\[13\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Bell1). Many widely used practical applications of statistical natural language processing, such as document retrieval based on keywords, also exploit dynamic patterns in word statistics [\[10\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Baayen1), [\[14\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Church1), [\[15\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Katz1). Particularly important for these applications, and also noticed in different contexts [\[16\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Montemurro1)–[\[21\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Serrano1), is the non-uniform distribution of content words through a text, suggesting that connections to the previous discoveries about inter-event distributions may be revealed through a systematic investigation of the recurrence times of different words.

With the rise of the Internet, large records of spontaneous and collective language are now available for scientific inquiry [\[22\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Watts1)–[\[24\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Lambiotte1), allowing statistical questions about language to be investigated with an unprecedented precision. At the same time, large-scale text mining and document classification is of ever-increasing importance [\[25\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Nigam1). The primary datasets used in our study are USENET discussion groups available through Google ([http://groups.google.com](http://groups.google.com/)). These exemplify spontaneous linguistic interactions in large communities over a long period of time. We first focus on the ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e001) words that occurred more than ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e002) times between Sept. 1986 and Mar. 2008 in a (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e003)\-word) discussion group, talk.origins. The data were collated chronologically, maintaining the thread structure (see [Text S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s001), _Databases_).

Here, we show that long-time word recurrence patterns follow a stretched exponential distribution, owing to bursts and lulls in word usage. We focus on time scales that exceed the scale of _syntactic_ relations, and the burstiness of the words is driven by their semantics (that is, by what they mean). The burstiness of physical events and socially contextualized choices makes words more bursty than an exponential distribution. However, we show that words are typically less bursty than other human activities [\[26\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Vzquez1) due to their _logicality_ or _permutability_ [\[27\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-vanBenthem1), [\[28\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-vonFintel1), technical constructs of formal semantics that index the extent to which the meanings and usage of words are stable over changes in the discourse context. Our quantitative analysis of the empirical data confirms the inverse relationship between burstiness and permutability. The model we develop to explain these observations shares the generative spirit of local (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e004)\-gram) and weakly non-local models of text classification and generation [\[29\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Shannon1)–[\[31\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Ron1). However it focuses on long time-scales, picking up at temporal scales where studies of local predictability and coherence leave off [\[13\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Bell1). We verify the generality of our main findings using different databases, including books of different genres and a series of political debates.

## Methods

We are interested in the temporal distribution of each word ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e005). All words are enumerated in order of appearance, ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e006), where ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e007) plays the role of the time along the text. The recurrence time ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e008) is defined by the number of words between two successive uses (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e009) and ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e010)) of word ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e011) (plus one). For instance, the first appearances of the word _the_ in the abstract above are at ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e012) leading to a sequence of recurrence times ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e013). We are interested in the distribution ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e014) of ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e015), ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e016). The mean recurrence time, called by Zipf the wavelength of the word [\[7\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Zipf1), is given by ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e017) [\[2\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Bunde1) (hereafter we drop ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e018) from our notation). It is mathematically convenient to consider ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e019) to be a continuous time variable (an assumption that is justified by our interested in ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e020)) and to use the cumulative probability density function defined by ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e021), which satisfies ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e022) and ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e023).

The first point of interest is how the distribution ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e024) \[or ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e025)\] deviates from the exponential distribution![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e026)(1)where ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e027) leads to ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e028). Deviations are caused by the way that people choose their words in context. Numerous studies, as reviewed in Ref. [\[32\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Tanenhaus1), already demonstrate that the language users dynamically modify their use of nouns and noun phrases as a function of the linguistic and external context. We analyze such modifications for all types of words.

Both words have ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e032) but are linguistically quite different: while _theory_ is a common noun, _also_ is an adverb that functions semantically as an operator. The deviation from the Poisson prediction (1) is apparent in [Fig. 1(a–c)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g001): ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e033) is larger than the exponential distribution for distances ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e034) both much shorter and much longer than ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e035), while it is smaller for ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e036). Both words exhibit a most probable recurrence time ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e037) and a monotonically decaying distribution ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e038) for larger times \[[Fig. 1(a)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g001)\]. Comparing the insets in [Fig. 1(b)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g001), one sees that the occurrences of _theory_ are clustered close to each other in a phenomenon known as burstiness [\[6\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Goh1), [\[14\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Church1), [\[15\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Katz1), [\[19\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Sarkar1), [\[21\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Serrano1). Due to burstiness, the frequency of the word _theory_ estimated from a small sample would differ a great deal as a function of exactly where the sample was drawn. Similar but lesser deviations are observed for the word _also_.

Central to our discussion, [Fig. 1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g001) shows that the distributions of both words can be well described by the single free parameter ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e060) of the stretched exponential distribution![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e061)(2)where ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e062) is obtained by imposing ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e063), ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e064) is the Gamma function, and ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e065). Distribution (2), also known as Weibull distribution, and similar stretched exponential distributions describe a variety of phenomena [\[6\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Goh1), [\[23\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Wu1), [\[33\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Laherrere1)–[\[35\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Redner1), including the recurrence time between extreme events in time series with long-term correlations [\[2\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Bunde1), [\[36\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Santhanam1). The stretched exponential (2) is more skewed than the simple exponential distribution (1), which corresponds to the limiting case ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e066), but less skewed than a power law, which is approached for ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e067).

A crucial test for the claim that an empirical distribution ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e068) follows a stretched exponential ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e069) is to represent ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e070) as a function of ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e071) in a double logarithmic plot [\[2\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Bunde1). The straight line behavior for almost three decades shown in [Fig. 1(b)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g001), which is illustrative of the words in our datasets, provides strong evidence for the stretched exponential scaling (spam-related deviations for long ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e072) are discussed in [Text S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s001), _Databases_). This is a clear advance over the closest precedents to our results: (i) In Ref. [\[8\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Zipf2) Zipf proposed a power-law decay, which would appear as an horizontal line in [Fig. 1b](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g001). (ii) Refs. [\[14\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Church1), [\[15\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Katz1) compare two non-stationary Poisson processes for predicting the counts of words in documents (see [Text S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s001), _Counting Distribution_); (iii) Ref. [\[19\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Sarkar1) proposes a non-homogeneous Poisson process for recurrence times, using a mixture of two exponentials with a total of four free parameters; (iv) Ref. [\[37\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Hrebicek1) uses the Zipf-Alekseev distribution ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e073), which we found to underestimate the decay rate for large ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e074) and to leave larger residuals than our fittings (see [Text S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s001), _Zipf-Alekseev Distribution_). The stretched exponential distribution was found to describe the time between usages of words in Blogs and RSS feeds in Ref. [\[24\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Lambiotte1). However, time was measured as actual time and the same distribution was found for different types of words, suggesting that their observations are driven by the bursty update of webpages, a related but different effect. More strongly related to our study is Ref. [\[5\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Malmgren1)'s analysis of email activity, in which a non-homogeneous Poisson process captures the way one email can trigger the next.

### Generative Model

Motivated by the successful description of the stretched exponential distribution (2), we search for a generative stochastic process that can model word usage. We consider the inverse frequency ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e075) as given and focus on describing how the words are distributed throughout the text. We assume that our text (abstractly regarded as arbitrarily long) is generated by a well-defined stationary stochastic process with finite ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e076) for the words of interest. We further assume that the probability ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e077) of using the word ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e078) depends only on the distance ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e079) since the last occurrence of the word. The latter means that we are modeling the word usage as a _renewal process_ [\[34\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Cox1), [\[36\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Santhanam1). The distribution of recurrence times is then given by the (joint) probability of having the word at distance ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e080) and not having this word for ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e081):![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e082)The cumulative distribution function is written as![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e083)(3)The time dependent probability ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e084), also known as _hazard function_, can be obtained empirically as ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e085) (see [Text S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s001), _Hazard Function_). Equation (3) reduces to the exponential distribution (1) for a time independent probability ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e086). The stretched exponential distribution (2) is obtained from (3) by asserting that [\[34\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Cox1), [\[36\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Santhanam1), [\[38\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-McShane1)![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e087)(4)This assertion means that in our model, the probability of using a word decays as a power law since the last use of that word. This is further justified by the power-law behavior of ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e088) determined directly from the empirical data, as shown in [Fig. 1(c)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g001) and [Text S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s001), Fig. 9, and is in agreement with results from mathematical psychology [\[39\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Wixted1), [\[40\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Anderson1) and information retrieval [\[40\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Anderson1). The Weibull renewal process we propose can be analyzed formally as a particular instance of a doubly stochastic Poisson process [\[41\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Yannaros1).

Our model is illustrated in [Fig. 1(d)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g001) and can be interpreted as a bag-of-words with memory that accounts for the burstiness of word usage. This model does not reproduce the positive correlations between ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e089) and ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e090) [\[2\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Bunde1), [\[6\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Goh1), [\[20\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-AlvarezLacalle1), which are usually small (less than ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e091)% for ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e092)) but decay slowly with ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e093) (see [Text S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s001), _Correlation in_ ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e094)). These correlations quantify the extent to which the renewal model is a good approximation of the actual generative process, and show that the burstiness of words exists not only as a departure of ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e095) from the exponential distribution, but also as a clustering of small (large) ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e096) [\[6\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Goh1) (see [Text S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s001), _Independence of_ ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e097)). The advantage of the renewal description is that the model (i) can be substantiated to a vast literature describing power-law decay of memory in agreement with Eq. (4), see Refs. [\[39\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Wixted1), [\[40\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Anderson1) and references therein, and (ii) fully determines the dynamics (allowing, e.g., the precise derivation of counting distributions [\[38\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-McShane1), which are used in applications to document classification [\[14\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Church1), [\[15\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Katz1) and information retrieval [\[40\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Anderson1)).

### Word Dependence

We have seen in [Fig. 1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g001) that the word-dependent deviation from the exponential distribution is encapsulated in the parameter ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e098): the smaller the ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e099) for any given word, the larger the deviation (see [Text S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s001), _Deviation from the Exponential Distribution_). Next we investigate the dominant effects that determine the value of the parameter ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e100) of a word. Previous research has observed that frequent _function_ words (such as conjunctions and determiners) usually are closer to the random (Poisson) prediction while less frequent _content_ words (particularly names and common nouns) are more bursty. These observations were quantified using: (i) an entropic analysis of texts [\[16\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Montemurro1); (ii) the variance of the sequence of recurrence times [\[17\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Ortuo1); (iii) the recurrence time distribution [\[19\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Sarkar1), [\[42\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Corral1); and (iv) the related distribution of the number of occurrences of words per document [\[14\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Church1), [\[15\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Katz1). Because we have a large database and do not bin the datastream into documents, we are able to go beyond these insightful works and systematically examine frequency and linguistic status as factors in word burstiness.

Our large database allows a detailed analysis of words that, despite being in the same frequency range, have very different statistical behavior. For instance, in the range ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e101), words with high ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e102) (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e103)) include _once, certainly, instead, yet, give, try, makes_, and _seem_; the few words with ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e104) include _design, selection, intelligent_, and _Wilkins_. Corroborating Ref. [\[14\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Church1), it is evident that words with low ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e105) better characterize the discourse topic. However, these examples also show that the distinction between function words and content words cannot be explanatory. For instance, many content words, such as the adverbs and verbs of mental representation in the list just above, have ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e106) values as high as many function words. Here we obtain a deeper level of explanation by drawing on tools from formal semantics, specifically on type theory [\[27\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-vanBenthem1), [\[43\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Montague1), [\[44\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Partee1), and on dynamic theories of semantics [\[45\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Heim1), [\[46\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Kamp1), which model how words and sentences update the discourse context over time. We use semantics rather than syntax because syntax governs how words are combined into sentences, and we are interested in much longer time scales over which syntactic relations are not defined. Type theory establishes a scale from simple entities (e.g., proper nouns) to high type words (e.g., words that cannot be described using first-order logic, including intensional expressions and operators). Simplifying the technical literature in the interests of good sample sizes and coding reliability, we define a ladder of four semantic classes, as listed in [Table 1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-t001).

In [Fig. 2](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g002), we report our systematical analysis of the recurrence time distribution of all ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e112) words that appeared more than ten thousand times in our database (for word-specific results see [Table S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s002)). We find a wide range of values for the burstiness parameter ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e113) \[![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e114), [Fig. 2(a,b)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g002)\] and the stretched exponential distribution describes well most of the words \[![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e115), [Fig. 2(c)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g002)\]. The Class-specific results displayed in [Fig. 2(a–c)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g002) show that words of all classes are accurately described by the same statistical model over a wide range of scales, a strong indication of a universal process governing word usage at these scales. We are now in a position to discuss why burstiness depends on semantic Class. A straw man theory would seek to derive the burstiness of referring expressions directly from the burstiness of their referents. The limitations of such a theory are obvious: _Oxygen_ is a very bursty word in our database (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e157)) though oxygen is ubiquitous. A more careful observer would connect the burstiness of words to the human decisions to perform activities related to the words. For instance, the recurrence time between sending emails is known to approximately follow a power law [\[3\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Barabsi1), [\[5\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Malmgren1). However, in our database the word _email_ is significantly closer to the exponential (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e158)) than a power law would predict (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e159)). Indeed, a defining characteristic of human language is the ability to refer to entities and events that are not present in the immediate reality [\[48\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Hockett1). These nontrivial connections between language and the world are investigated in semantics. An insight on the problem of word usage can be obtained from Ref. [\[27\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-vanBenthem1), which establishes that the meaning and applicability of words with great _logicality_ remains invariant under _permutations_ of alternatives for the entities and relations specified in the constructions in which they appear. Here we consider permutability to be proportional to the semantic Classes of [Table 1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-t001). As a long discourse unfolds exploring different constructions, we expect words with higher permutability (higher semantic Class) to be more homogeneously distributed throughout the discourse and therefore have higher ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e160) (be less bursty). Critical to this explanation is the fact that human language manipulates representations of abstract operators and mental states [\[49\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Hauser1). However, the overt statistics of recurrence times do not need to be learned word by word. It seems more likely that they are an epiphenomenal result of the differential contextualization of word meanings. The fact that the behavior of almost all words deviate from a Poisson process to at least some extent, indicates that the permutability and usage of almost all words are contextually restricted to some degree, whether by their intrinsic meaning or by their social connotations.

In [Fig. 3](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g003) we verify our main results using databases of different sizes and characterized by different levels of formality. We analyzed a second example of a USENET group (U), a series of political debates (D), two novels (S,W), and a technical book (P) (for word-specific results see [Table S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s002)). The stretched exponential provides a close fit for frequent words in these datasets \[[Fig. 3(a,c)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g003)\], and a wide and smoothly varying range of ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e161)s is observed in each case \[[Fig. 3(b)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g003)\]. The technical book exhibits lower ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e162) values, which can be attributed to the predominance of specific scientific terms. These datasets include examples of texts differing by almost four orders of magnitudes in size, generated by a single author (books), a few authors (debates) or a large number of authors (USENET), in writing and speech (e.g., books vs. debates), and in different languages (e.g., novels), indicating that the stretched exponential scaling is robust with regard to sample size, number of authors, language mode, and language.

### Conclusions

The quest for statistical laws in language has been driven both by applications in text mining and document retrieval, and by the desire for foundational understanding of humans as agents and participants in the world. Taking texts as examples of extended discourse, we combined these research agendas by showing that word meanings are directly related to their recurrence distributions via the permutability of concepts across discourse contexts. Our model for generating long-term recurrence patterns of words, a bag-of-words model with memory, is stationary and uniformly applicable to words of all parts of speech and semantic types. A word's position along the range in the memory parameter in the model, ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e184), effectively captures its position in between a power-law and an exponential distribution, thus capturing its degree of contextual anchoring. Our results agree with Ref. [\[49\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Hauser1) in emphasizing both the specific ability to learn abstract operators and the broader conceptual-intentional system as components in the human capability for language and in its use in the flow of discourse.

Analogies between communicative dynamics and social dynamics more generally are suggested by the recent documentation of heavy-tailed distributions in many other human driven activities [\[3\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Barabsi1), [\[5\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Malmgren1), [\[26\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Vzquez1). They indicate that tracing linguistic activities in the ever larger digital databases of human communications can be a most promising tool for tracing human and social dynamics [\[22\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Watts1). The stretched exponential form for recurrence distributions that derives from our model and the empirical finding it embodies are thus expected to also find applicability in other areas of human endeavor.
