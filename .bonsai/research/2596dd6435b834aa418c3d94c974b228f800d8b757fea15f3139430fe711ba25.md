## Compressed

### 3.1 Datasets.

##### Human Study.

We conducted a human user study of 100 participants (demographic information in [Section A.8]) to understand how people leverage LLMs to write an argumentative essay. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of two conditions: (1) the control group (n\=45n=45), where participants were not allowed to use LLMs, or (2) an AI-assisted condition (n\=55n=55), where participants were allowed to use an embedded LLM (gpt-4o-mini) in whatever way they chose while writing.

To evaluate the impact of LLM intervention, each participant completed a pre-study and post-study questionnaire to measure shifts in attitudes toward LLMs, their own creativity, and the alignment of model outputs with their writing preferences.

Before running the full experiment, we first conducted an initial pilot study (n\=8n=8) to test the protocol. Analysis of this data revealed two distinct behaviors among participants in the experimental condition, with access to the LLM. Roughly half of the participants either abstained from LLM usage entirely or utilized the tool strictly for peripheral information-seeking or critiques rather than content generation, while the other half used the LLM extensively. As the purpose of the study is to understand how naturalistic interactions with LLMs alter writing, the goal is not to coerce the users into a specific LLM usage pattern. Therefore, for our primary study (n\=100n=100), we categorized into either the LLM-Influenced (minimal usage to generate text) or LLM (extensive generative use) conditions. Concretely, a participant is categorized as LLM-influenced if they self-report to have generated less than 40% of the text with an LLM, and when we cross-reference their essay with their conversation with the LLM, we find this to be an accurate statement. In the human user study presented in this paper (n\=100n=100), 28 out of 55 users were classified as LLM-Influenced, with 12 out of 55 refusing to use the LLM to generate their essay at all.

ArgRewrite-v2. All participants in the study developed an initial argumentative essay draft (D1) for or against self-driving cars that could serve as an op-ed piece in a local newspaper. Each D1 draft was then provided with feedback from a human expert on how to improve the essay, which included both coarse-grained (surface vs content) and fine-grained (e.g., claim, evidence, reasoning, word usage, precision, etc.) feedback. Specifically, we prompted three production LLMs from the top frontier AI labs (gpt-5-mini, gemini-2.5-flash, claude-haiku) to edit the original human-drafted D1 essays in the ArgRewrite-v2 dataset. The LLMs condition on both the D1 draft and the human-written feedback to produce the edits. We compare the LLM-generated D2 drafts with the human-written D2, to counterfactually analyze how much LLMs change the essays relative to what a human would do if they were not influenced by LLMs.

Because prompting an LLM is critical to its effective performance ([Ggaliwango et al., 2023]), for this experiment we needed to provide realistic prompts that would reflect how people actually ask LLMs to revise their writing. Our user study showed that people employ LLMs for a variety of use cases: generating ideas and arguments, expanding their own ideas from existing human-written text, writing a first paragraph and asking the LLM to finish the rest, asking the LLM to review the human-written essay, and asking the LLM to write the entire essay. Based on this analysis, we prompted LLMs to perform five revision types: (1) general revision to comprehensively improve the essay; (2) minimal edits that make only necessary corrections; (3) grammar revisions that fix surface errors without changing content; (4) completion revisions to finish incomplete essays; and (5) expansion to elaborate on existing ideas. For each revision type, we provided the LLM with the expert human-written feedback on the original D1 draft from the ArgRewrite-v2 dataset, which provides further instruction about how the essay should be revised, and helps to mitigate the LLM-induced homogenization ([Figure 18] shows the magnitude of the effects with and without conditioning on expert revision instruction).

Peer Reviews. To analyze how LLM use in the wild may shift the claims made in real communication, we analyze peer reviews from the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) (gonzalez2024learning). Of the 75,000 reviews written for ICLR 2026, over 21% were found to be LLM-generated, with an additional 39% of papers that used LLMs to edit or generate parts of the text ([Emi, 2025]). Our analysis compares human-written peer reviews with those classified as ‘LLM-generated’. We selected only those papers with one review written entirely by a human and one written entirely by an LLM to ensure there is no bias in our sampling of human or LLM reviews.

## Detailed

### 3.1 Datasets.

##### Human Study.

We conducted a human user study of 100 participants (demographic information in [Section A.8](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#A1.SS8 "A.8 User Study Demographics ‣ Appendix A User Study Details ‣ How LLMs Distort Our Written Language")) to understand how people leverage LLMs to write an argumentative essay. We chose the essay prompt,“Does money lead to happiness?”, since it is relatable to participants from all backgrounds. Participants were recruited from Prolific. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of two conditions: (1) the control group (n\=45n=45), where participants were not allowed to use LLMs, or (2) an AI-assisted condition (n\=55n=55), where participants were allowed to use an embedded LLM (gpt-4o-mini) in whatever way they chose while writing. For the AI-assisted group, we recorded the full interaction history with the LLM as well as the final draft written with LLM assistance. We recruit participants who are native English speakers and residing in the United States through Prolific to ensure that country or native language is not a confounding variable in our study. More information about recruitment and user study information can be found at [Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#A1 "Appendix A User Study Details ‣ How LLMs Distort Our Written Language").

To evaluate the impact of LLM intervention, each participant completed a pre-study and post-study questionnaire to measure shifts in attitudes toward LLMs, their own creativity, and the alignment of model outputs with their writing preferences. These self-reported ratings allow us to measure if human preferences are being met when collaborating with LLMs. We provide the pre-study and post-study questions in [Section A.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#A1.SS2 "A.2 User Study Instructions. ‣ Appendix A User Study Details ‣ How LLMs Distort Our Written Language"), and details on compensation, recruitment process, and duration of the study in [Section A.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#A1.SS1 "A.1 User Study Recruitment Process, Compensation and Duration ‣ Appendix A User Study Details ‣ How LLMs Distort Our Written Language"). All procedures were approved by our Institutional Review Board (IRB).

ArgRewrite-v2. ArgRewrite-v2 ([Kashefi et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#bib.bib8)) is a dataset of argumentative writing revisions collected from 86 university students. Importantly, the essays were written in 2021 and the dataset was released in 2022 before the release of ChatGPT ([OpenAI, 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#bib.bib57)). Thus, the writing pre-dates the widespread adoption of LLM-based writing assistants. All participants in the study developed an initial argumentative essay draft (D1) for or against self-driving cars that could serve as an op-ed piece in a local newspaper. Each D1 draft was then provided with feedback from a human expert on how to improve the essay, which included both coarse-grained (surface vs content) and fine-grained (e.g., claim, evidence, reasoning, word usage, precision, etc.) feedback. In response, participants revised the initial draft to form the second draft (D2). To enable a comparative analysis, we then construct a dataset of LLM-generated D2 drafts. Peer Reviews. To analyze how LLM use in the wild may shift the claims made in real communication, we analyze peer reviews from the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR) (gonzalez2024learning). In the ICLR review process, not only are reviewers prohibited from using AI to generate reviews, but their names and reviews are visible to other top scientists in their field who act as “area chairs.” Thus, to protect their professional reputation, reviewers have a strong incentive to both conceal their use of AI, and to create high-quality reviews. Of the 75,000 reviews written for ICLR 2026, over 21% were found to be LLM-generated, with an additional 39% of papers that used LLMs to edit or generate parts of the text ([Emi, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#bib.bib11)). Detection was performed using the Pangram AI tool, a classifier with a low false positive rate ([Emi and Spero, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#bib.bib48))11 1 It is possible for this tool to have errors and to introduce correlations.. Our analysis compares human-written peer reviews with those classified as ‘LLM-generated’. We asked whether LLM-generated reviews assign different scores, and also whether they actually focus on different scientific criteria. To determine this, we use an LLM-as-a-Judge classifier to identify the strengths and weaknesses of 18k reviews from ICLR 2026 from 9k papers. We selected only those papers with one review written entirely by a human and one written entirely by an LLM to ensure there is no bias in our sampling of human or LLM reviews.
