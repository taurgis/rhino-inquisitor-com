## Detailed

### Different Databases

In [Fig. 3](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g003) we verify our main results using databases of different sizes and characterized by different levels of formality. We analyzed a second example of a USENET group (U), a series of political debates (D), two novels (S,W), and a technical book (P) (for word-specific results see [Table S1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678.s002)). The stretched exponential provides a close fit for frequent words in these datasets \[[Fig. 3(a,c)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g003)\], and a wide and smoothly varying range of ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e161)s is observed in each case \[[Fig. 3(b)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-g003)\]. The technical book exhibits lower ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e162) values, which can be attributed to the predominance of specific scientific terms. These datasets include examples of texts differing by almost four orders of magnitudes in size, generated by a single author (books), a few authors (debates) or a large number of authors (USENET), in writing and speech (e.g., books vs. debates), and in different languages (e.g., novels), indicating that the stretched exponential scaling is robust with regard to sample size, number of authors, language mode, and language.
