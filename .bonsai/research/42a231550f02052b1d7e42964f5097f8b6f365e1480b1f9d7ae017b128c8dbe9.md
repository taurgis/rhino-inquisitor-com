## 1\. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly shaping online discourse and are being integrated into everyday applications to improve user productivity and experience. Writing, in particular, has become a prominent area where LLMs offer substantial assistance, supporting tasks such as scientific writing ([Gero et al., 2021]), storytelling ([Singh et al., 2023]), and journalism ([Petridis et al., 2023]). These applications provide inline writing suggestions, which users can accept or reject in real-time. Such AI writing suggestions have become integral to email clients (e.g., Gmail Smart Compose ([Chen et al., 2019])), note-taking applications (e.g., Notion), and word processors (e.g., Google Docs).

While LLM-embedded applications like text autocomplete have gained widespread global adoption, these technologies also carry significant risks, such as perpetuating harmful stereotypes about marginalized groups and underserved communities. Additionally, research has revealed that LLMs prioritize Western norms and values in their interactions ([Johnson et al., 2022]; [Cao et al., 2023]), resulting in representational harms for diverse non-Western cultures ([Shelby et al., 2023]; [Basu et al., 2023]; [Qadri et al., 2023]).

While explicit cultural stereotyping shown by prior work is deeply problematic, an even more insidious issue lies in the subtle biases LLMs can introduce through their suggestions. For example, recent work shows that autocomplete suggestions can change users’ language ([Hohenstein et al., 2023]), writing ([Poddar et al., 2023]), and even attitudes about social topics ([Jakesch et al., 2023]; [Williams-Ceci et al., 2024]). Furthermore, unlike chat-based applications such as ChatGPT, LLM-embedded applications do not allow users to prompt and fine-tune the model to suit their cultural preferences. This creates situations where users and AI may have different cultural values, potentially leading to conflicts over whose norms should be expressed. While prior studies have explored cultural harms in LLMs using open-ended prompts, no research has yet examined such cultural clashes in embedded AI applications. In this paper, we examine how AI suggestions influence user-generated content when the AI and users share the same cultural identity versus when they do not.

*   RQ1:
    
    Does writing with a Western-centric AI provide greater benefits to users from Western cultures, compared to those from non-Western cultures?
    
*   RQ2:
    
    Does writing with a Western-centric AI homogenize the writing styles of non-Western users toward Western styles?
    

To answer these questions, we conducted a cross-cultural experiment with 118118 users from India and the US recruited through Prolific, an online crowdsourcing platform. Each participant was randomly assigned either to the AI condition receiving autocomplete suggestions from GPT-4o while writing or the No AI condition writing organically without AI assistance. Subsequently, we compared the essays written by participants in the four experimental groups (Indian and American users writing with and without AI suggestions).

Our analysis yielded two key results. In response to RQ2, we found that AI caused Indian participants to write more like Americans, thereby homogenizing writing toward Western styles and diminishing nuances that differentiate cultural expression. Worryingly, AI influences not just what is written (e.g., shifting preferences toward Western cultural artifacts such as food items), but also more ingrained elements of how it’s written, silently erasing non-Western styles of cultural expression. For instance, with AI suggestions, Indians lose cultural nuance and describe their own food and festivals from a Western gaze.

*   •
    
    We present the first cross-cultural analysis of AI writing suggestions, providing concrete evidence of higher productivity gains for Western users compared to non-Western users.
    
*   •
    
    We show how cultural homogenization occurs when non-Western users write with Western-centric AI models.
    
*   •
    
    We discuss the potential harms of cross-cultural homogenization and propose strategies to address the harms of cultural imperialism and linguistic singularity.

## 1\. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly shaping online discourse and are being integrated into everyday applications to improve user productivity and experience. These include chatbots that aid in content comprehension11 1 [AI Assistant in Adobe Acrobat](https://www.adobe.com/acrobat/generative-ai-pdf.html), [AskYourPDF](https://askyourpdf.com/), code completion systems that streamline programming22 2 [GitHub Copilot](https://github.com/features/copilot), [Cursor](https://cursor.com/), and autocomplete tools that facilitate faster writing ([Chen et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib17)). Writing, in particular, has become a prominent area where LLMs offer substantial assistance, supporting tasks such as scientific writing ([Gero et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib31)), storytelling ([Singh et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib80)), and journalism ([Petridis et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib66)). These applications provide inline writing suggestions, which users can accept or reject in real-time. Such AI writing suggestions have become integral to email clients (e.g., Gmail Smart Compose ([Chen et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib17))), note-taking applications (e.g., Notion), and word processors (e.g., Google Docs). Their growing utility is evident from their integration into popular web browsers like Google Chrome and Microsoft Edge, which now offer autocomplete as a native feature, enabled by default33 3 See announcements: [Microsoft Edge](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/edge/features/text-prediction), [Google Chrome](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/14582048).

While LLM-embedded applications like text autocomplete have gained widespread global adoption, these technologies also carry significant risks, such as perpetuating harmful stereotypes about marginalized groups and underserved communities. For instance, LLMs have been shown to depict Muslims as terrorists ([Abid et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib2)) and reinforce negative stereotypes about disabled individuals ([Venkit et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib86); [Gadiraju et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib28)). Additionally, research has revealed that LLMs prioritize Western norms and values in their interactions ([Johnson et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib45); [Cao et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib15)), resulting in representational harms for diverse non-Western cultures ([Shelby et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib78); [Basu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib10); [Qadri et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib69)).

While explicit cultural stereotyping shown by prior work is deeply problematic, an even more insidious issue lies in the subtle biases LLMs can introduce through their suggestions. For example, recent work shows that autocomplete suggestions can change users’ language ([Hohenstein et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib41)), writing ([Poddar et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib67)), and even attitudes about social topics ([Jakesch et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib44); [Williams-Ceci et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib87)). Furthermore, unlike chat-based applications such as ChatGPT, LLM-embedded applications do not allow users to prompt and fine-tune the model to suit their cultural preferences. This creates situations where users and AI may have different cultural values, potentially leading to conflicts over whose norms should be expressed. While prior studies have explored cultural harms in LLMs using open-ended prompts, no research has yet examined such cultural clashes in embedded AI applications. In this paper, we examine how AI suggestions influence user-generated content when the AI and users share the same cultural identity versus when they do not. In particular, we ask:

*   RQ1:
    
    Does writing with a Western-centric AI provide greater benefits to users from Western cultures, compared to those from non-Western cultures?
    
*   RQ2:
    
    Does writing with a Western-centric AI homogenize the writing styles of non-Western users toward Western styles?
    

To answer these questions, we conducted a cross-cultural experiment with 118118 users from India and the US recruited through Prolific, an online crowdsourcing platform. Participants from both cultures were asked to complete writing tasks in English. We designed the task prompts using Hofstede’s Cultural Onion framework ([Hofstede, 1991](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib40)), which allowed us to elicit cultural practices ranging from explicit (e.g., food) to implicit (e.g., rituals). Each participant was randomly assigned either to the AI condition receiving autocomplete suggestions from GPT-4o while writing or the No AI condition writing organically without AI assistance. Subsequently, we compared the essays written by participants in the four experimental groups (Indian and American users writing with and without AI suggestions).

Our analysis yielded two key results. In response to RQ1, we found that while AI boosts productivity for both Indian and American participants, the gains are higher for American participants. This discrepancy raises concerns about quality-of-service harms ([Shelby et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib78)) for non-Western users, wherein they need to put in more effort to achieve similar benefits. In response to RQ2, we found that AI caused Indian participants to write more like Americans, thereby homogenizing writing toward Western styles and diminishing nuances that differentiate cultural expression. Worryingly, AI influences not just what is written (e.g., shifting preferences toward Western cultural artifacts such as food items), but also more ingrained elements of how it’s written, silently erasing non-Western styles of cultural expression. For instance, with AI suggestions, Indians lose cultural nuance and describe their own food and festivals from a Western gaze. We discuss the implications of these cultural harms from a colonial lens and propose strategies to mitigate them. Overall, we make the following contributions to the HCI and AI communities:
