### 4.6 The Use of LLMs for Writing Increases Emotional Language

We examined whether LLM edits change the distribution of emotions present in text using the NRC Lexicon ([Mohammad and Turney, 2013]) on the ArgRewrite-v2 dataset. [Figure 9] shows the change in emotional word density for general and minimal revisions with and without expert feedback. We find human edits make minimal affective changes (black), with adjustments typically under 5% for any emotion category compared to the initial human draft. On the other hand, LLMs increase the amount of emotional language used in general, with LLMs expressing slightly more words relating to positivity and trust labels in the essay. This pattern suggests that LLMs systematically reframe arguments in more positive, optimistic terms, even when the original human text may have been critical or skeptical. Interestingly, we also find that without expert human feedback guiding the LLM edits, the emotional shifts across all categories relative to the initial human draft are even more drastic, reinforcing the observation that LLMs increase emotional language in the essay.

LLMs increase positive sentiment (37-54%) and trust-related language (17-53%), while also simultaneously increasing negative sentiment (24-38%). This indicates increases in the use of emotional language, regardless of which LLM model is used.

## Detailed

### 4.6 The Use of LLMs for Writing Increases Emotional Language

We examined whether LLM edits change the distribution of emotions present in text using the NRC Lexicon ([Mohammad and Turney, 2013](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#bib.bib38)) on the ArgRewrite-v2 dataset. [Figure 9](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#S4.F9 "In 4.6 The Use of LLMs for Writing Increases Emotional Language ‣ 4 Results ‣ How LLMs Distort Our Written Language") shows the change in emotional word density for general and minimal revisions with and without expert feedback. We find human edits make minimal affective changes (black), with adjustments typically under 5% for any emotion category compared to the initial human draft. On the other hand, LLMs increase the amount of emotional language used in general, with LLMs expressing slightly more words relating to positivity and trust labels in the essay. This pattern suggests that LLMs systematically reframe arguments in more positive, optimistic terms, even when the original human text may have been critical or skeptical. For essays about self-driving cars (the ArgRewrite-v2 topic), this could mean downplaying concerns about safety, job displacement, or ethical issues in favor of enthusiasm about technological progress, as shown in [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#S2.F2 "In The Effect of AI on Institutions. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ How LLMs Distort Our Written Language"), where the LLM removes mention of the drawbacks of self-driving cars. Interestingly, we also find that without expert human feedback guiding the LLM edits, the emotional shifts across all categories relative to the initial human draft are even more drastic, reinforcing the observation that LLMs increase emotional language in the essay. We find differences between models, with claude-haiku showing less pronounced shifts with expert feedback. Further results with different LLM-editing conditions and LLMs found in Appendix [B.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161#A2.SS7 "B.7 Emotional Shift ‣ Appendix B ArgRewrite-v2 Analysis ‣ How LLMs Distort Our Written Language").
