We are now in a position to discuss why burstiness depends on semantic Class. A more careful observer would connect the burstiness of words to the human decisions to perform activities related to the words. An insight on the problem of word usage can be obtained from Ref. [\[27\]], which establishes that the meaning and applicability of words with great _logicality_ remains invariant under _permutations_ of alternatives for the entities and relations specified in the constructions in which they appear. As a long discourse unfolds exploring different constructions, we expect words with higher permutability (higher semantic Class) to be more homogeneously distributed throughout the discourse and therefore have higher (be less bursty). However, the overt statistics of recurrence times do not need to be learned word by word.

We are now in a position to discuss why burstiness depends on semantic Class. A straw man theory would seek to derive the burstiness of referring expressions directly from the burstiness of their referents. The limitations of such a theory are obvious: _Oxygen_ is a very bursty word in our database (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e157)) though oxygen is ubiquitous. A more careful observer would connect the burstiness of words to the human decisions to perform activities related to the words. For instance, the recurrence time between sending emails is known to approximately follow a power law [\[3\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Barabsi1), [\[5\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Malmgren1). However, in our database the word _email_ is significantly closer to the exponential (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e158)) than a power law would predict (![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e159)). Indeed, a defining characteristic of human language is the ability to refer to entities and events that are not present in the immediate reality [\[48\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Hockett1). These nontrivial connections between language and the world are investigated in semantics. An insight on the problem of word usage can be obtained from Ref. [\[27\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-vanBenthem1), which establishes that the meaning and applicability of words with great _logicality_ remains invariant under _permutations_ of alternatives for the entities and relations specified in the constructions in which they appear. Here we consider permutability to be proportional to the semantic Classes of [Table 1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone-0007678-t001). As a long discourse unfolds exploring different constructions, we expect words with higher permutability (higher semantic Class) to be more homogeneously distributed throughout the discourse and therefore have higher ![](article/file?type=thumbnail&id=10.1371/journal.pone.0007678.e160) (be less bursty). Critical to this explanation is the fact that human language manipulates representations of abstract operators and mental states [\[49\]](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0007678#pone.0007678-Hauser1). However, the overt statistics of recurrence times do not need to be learned word by word. It seems more likely that they are an epiphenomenal result of the differential contextualization of word meanings. The fact that the behavior of almost all words deviate from a Poisson process to at least some extent, indicates that the permutability and usage of almost all words are contextually restricted to some degree, whether by their intrinsic meaning or by their social connotations.
