##### Critical Failures (Appendix [E]).

The main results (Table [1]) average degradations across hundreds of simulations for each model, giving an impression of smooth degradation curves, with each interaction leading to a small amount of additional degradation. Table [9] summarizes the result of the analysis, reporting for each model the likelihood of a critical error after NN interactions, and the proportion of total error attributable to critical errors. Stronger models do not avoid small errors better; they delay critical failures and experience them in fewer interactions.

##### Deletion vs. Corruption (Appendix [F]).

So far, the paper primarily discusses overall degradation that occurs during simulation. To explore this further, we decompose model degradation into two components: deletion of content vs. corruption of existing content. For each domain, we count the structured elements (e.g., ingredients, steps) before and after a round trip: any reduction in count is attributed to deletion, and the remaining degradation is attributed to corruption. Analysis results are summarized for each model in Figure [7]. ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate"). We find that weaker models’ degradation originates primarily from content deletion, while frontier models’ degradation is attributable to corruption of content.

##### Critical Failures (Appendix [E](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#A5 "Appendix E Critical Error Analysis ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate")).

The main results (Table [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Results ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate")) average degradations across hundreds of simulations for each model, giving an impression of smooth degradation curves, with each interaction leading to a small amount of additional degradation. To look beyond this aggregate view, we analyzed the dynamics of individual relay simulations. We categorize each round-trip as introducing a critical failure if it led to a drop in score of at least 10%. Table [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#S5.T9 "Table 9 ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate") summarizes the result of the analysis, reporting for each model the likelihood of a critical error after NN interactions, and the proportion of total error attributable to critical errors. We find that models are not failing due to “death by a thousand cuts” (i.e., many small errors). Instead, they maintain near-perfect reconstruction in some rounds, and experience critical failures in a few rounds, typically losing 10-30+ points in a single round trip. These sparse critical failures explain about 80% of total document degradation we observe. Stronger models do not avoid small errors better; they delay critical failures and experience them in fewer interactions.

##### Deletion vs. Corruption (Appendix [F](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#A6 "Appendix F Deletion vs. Corruption Decomposition ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate")).

So far, the paper primarily discusses overall degradation that occurs during simulation. Yet, degradation can be caused by several underlying phenomena. To explore this further, we decompose model degradation into two components: deletion of content vs. corruption of existing content. For this analysis, we leverage the Domain Statistics component of domains in the benchmark (see Figure [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#S2.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 2.2.2 Domain-Specific Evaluation ‣ 2.2 Benchmark Construction ‣ 2 The DELEGATE-52 Benchmark ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate")). For each domain, we count the structured elements (e.g., ingredients, steps) before and after a round trip: any reduction in count is attributed to deletion, and the remaining degradation is attributed to corruption. Analysis results are summarized for each model in Figure [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#S5.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Critical Failures (Appendix ). ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate"). We find that weaker models’ degradation originates primarily from content deletion, while frontier models’ degradation is attributable to corruption of content.

Figure 8: Cohen’s dd effect sizes for document characteristics on scores.

##### Document Characteristics (Appendix [G](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#A7 "Appendix G Document Characteristics Analysis ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate")).

We analyzed how various document characteristics affect model performance, finding that models perform better in programmatic domains (Python, DBSchema) compared to natural language domains (e.g., Recipe, Fiction). Performance is also higher in domains with high repetitiveness and structural density (e.g., Molecule, Chess), and lower in domains with rich unrepeated vocabulary (e.g., Transit, Textile). This echoes prior findings that LLM performance is highest in programmatic or structured domains where verifiable rewards can be defined ([Suma and Dauncey, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#bib.bib92)). Through this lens, our work can be interpreted as a process to create verifiable rewards for a wide variety of domains, by building domain-specific parsing and evaluation.

##### Semantic Operations (Appendix [H](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#A8 "Appendix H Semantic Operation Analysis ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate")).

Each editing task in DELEGATE-52 was tagged with semantic operation tags that represent actions a model must take to successfully complete the task (such as sorting, merging, or string manipulation). The 11 semantic operations are listed in Figure [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#S5.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Document Characteristics (Appendix ). ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate") as well as a point-biserial correlation analysis between the presence of a tag and the reconstruction score of GPT 5.2 during a single round trip. We find editing tasks that require global document restructuring (e.g., split and merge, classification) are significantly harder than tasks involving local operations (e.g., string manipulation, referencing). In Appendix [H](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15597#A8 "Appendix H Semantic Operation Analysis ‣ LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate"), we also show that tasks that require coordinating multiple operations are more challenging than tasks that involve only one.
