### 5.3 Case Study and Failure Analysis

In this section, we further summarize the challenges of long-form evaluation revealed by LongJudgeBench and discuss potential improvements.

Misled by superficial coverage. Long-form outputs are often lengthy, well-structured, and rich in background knowledge, which can create an impression of sufficient coverage without truly addressing the user’s core question. In a computational chemistry case, the user asks how to simulate an external electric field in Gaussian for a single-atom catalyst system with uncertain molecular orientation. The key issue is not general external-field simulation, but how to handle the field direction under orientation uncertainty. Although the candidate response contains about 12K tokens and extensively discusses Gaussian Field syntax, VASP, and other general topics, it devotes only a few sentences to the core issue. Still, Qwen3-Max assigns an overall score of 9.26, while the human score is only 5.87. This case shows that LLM judges can mistake surface richness for substantive answers. A more reliable long-form judge should look beyond coverage and structure to assess whether the output truly addresses the user’s central need.

Scenario-specific concept misgrounding. Long-form generation tasks often involve complex domain-specific concepts, but the LLM judge may lack the conceptual discrimination needed for reliable evaluation. In a technical survey case, the user asks about the concrete implementation of Anthropic’s recently released Streamable HTTP. Although the candidate response is about 14K tokens long, it mainly discusses general SSE-based streaming in the Anthropic Messages API. The Qwen3-Max judge incorrectly treats Messages API streaming and Streamable HTTP as the same concept, assigning an overall score of 8.85, while the human score is only 5.37. Similarly, Table [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.01629#S3.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 3.4 Task Statistics ‣ 3 LongJudgeBench ‣ Benchmarking LLM-as-a-Judge for Long-Form Output Evaluation") further shows that LLM judges perform poorly on the MA medical-domain dataset, suggesting that it may struggle to apply domain-specific evaluation criteria in specialized scenarios. Future long-form judges need stronger domain concept disambiguation and better adaptation to different evaluation demands.

Other failures. LLM judges also exhibit some systematic biases and practical failures. As detailed in Appendix [B](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.01629#A2 "Appendix B Analysis Details ‣ Benchmarking LLM-as-a-Judge for Long-Form Output Evaluation"), their decisions can be affected by irrelevant factors such as candidate output order, indicating position bias. In addition, some judgments fail because of context-window overflow or safety-policy rejection, especially when long outputs are combined with references and rubrics or when inputs contain sensitive legal-domain content. These findings show that long-form evaluation is constrained not only by judgment accuracy, but also by robustness to bias, prompt complexity, and model safety behavior.
