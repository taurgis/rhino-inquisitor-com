### 2.1. Cultural Bias in AI Models

An emerging body of HCI and AI scholarship has examined the cultural composition of generative AI technologies ([Adilazuarda et al., 2024]), and found that these models center Western norms and values ([Cao et al., 2023]; [Tao et al., 2024]). For example, [Qadri et al. (2023)] showed that text-to-image models fail to generate cultural artifacts, amplify hegemonic cultural defaults, and perpetuate cultural tropes when depicting non-Western cultures. Even _within_ the West, these AI models tend to prioritize US-centric values and artifacts ([Basu et al., 2023]; [Schwöbel et al., 2023]; [Venkit et al., 2022]; [Johnson et al., 2022]). Together, these studies reveal the harms of culturally incongruent AI models ([Prabhakaran et al., 2022]). They underscore how data sourced from low-paid workers in non-Western regions is commodified to create models that inadequately serve these regions, a phenomenon known as data colonization ([Couldry and Mejias, 2019b]). Moreover, these systems reinforce Western cultural hegemony, perpetuate cultural erasure, and amplify stereotypes ([Qadri et al., 2023]), echoing the dynamics of colonial times and giving rise to AI colonialism. ([Hao, 2022]; [Tacheva and Ramasubramanian, 2023]).

Addressing these cultural biases is crucial not only to prevent representational, allocative, and quality-of-service harms ([Barocas et al., 2017]; [Shelby et al., 2023]; [Anuyah et al., 2023]) but also to avoid culture clashes that arise when the values embedded in AI models diverge from those of their users ([Prabhakaran et al., 2022]; [Hershcovich et al., 2022]).

The commercial growth has also driven a shift toward embedded AI applications, where the underlying models are obscured from users. We examine this culture clash that arises when users interact with embedded AI models that are culturally distant from them. To our knowledge, this is the first to reveal cultural homogenization effects when embedded AI features continue to be powered by culturally biased models.

## Detailed

### 2.1. Cultural Bias in AI Models

An emerging body of HCI and AI scholarship has examined the cultural composition of generative AI technologies ([Adilazuarda et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib3)), and found that these models center Western norms and values ([Cao et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib15); [Tao et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib84)). For example, [Qadri et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib69) showed that text-to-image models fail to generate cultural artifacts, amplify hegemonic cultural defaults, and perpetuate cultural tropes when depicting non-Western cultures. Even _within_ the West, these AI models tend to prioritize US-centric values and artifacts ([Basu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib10); [Schwöbel et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib75); [Venkit et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib86); [Johnson et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib45)). Furthermore, LLMs trained and probed in non-Western languages (e.g., Arabic) continue to show Western bias ([Naous et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib62); [Arora et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib7)). Recognizing these biases, recent work has proposed benchmark datasets to evaluate the multicultural knowledge of LLMs ([Chiu et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib18); [Rao et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib71)). Together, these studies reveal the harms of culturally incongruent AI models ([Prabhakaran et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib68)). They underscore how data sourced from low-paid workers in non-Western regions is commodified to create models that inadequately serve these regions, a phenomenon known as data colonization ([Couldry and Mejias, 2019b](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib21)). Moreover, these systems reinforce Western cultural hegemony, perpetuate cultural erasure, and amplify stereotypes ([Qadri et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib69)), echoing the dynamics of colonial times and giving rise to AI colonialism. ([Hao, 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib35); [Tacheva and Ramasubramanian, 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib82)).

Addressing these cultural biases is crucial not only to prevent representational, allocative, and quality-of-service harms ([Barocas et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib9); [Shelby et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib78); [Anuyah et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib5)) but also to avoid culture clashes that arise when the values embedded in AI models diverge from those of their users ([Prabhakaran et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib68); [Hershcovich et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib37)). Early work has acknowledged these challenges of value plurality, questioning what it means to imbue AI with human values when those values vary across cultures ([Tamkin et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib83)), and which values a model should prioritize when producing a singular output ([Johnson et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib45)). As generative AI technologies like LLMs are increasingly integrated into global products and services, these questions have become critically important.

The commercial growth has also driven a shift toward embedded AI applications, where the underlying models are obscured from users. This opacity makes it challenging to mitigate cultural biases through open-ended prompting or fine-tuning, thereby surfacing cultural clashes. We examine this culture clash that arises when users interact with embedded AI models that are culturally distant from them. To our knowledge, this is the first to reveal cultural homogenization effects when embedded AI features continue to be powered by culturally biased models.
