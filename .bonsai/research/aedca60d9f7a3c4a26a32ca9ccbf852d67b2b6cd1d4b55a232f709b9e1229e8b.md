## 6\. TTCW Implementation with LLMs as Assessors

Expert annotation such as the one we perform in Section [5] is costly: based on our evaluation protocol, evaluating a 1500-2500 word short story with a qualified expert costs $20, and requires roughly 30 minutes of the expert’s time. ## 6\. TTCW Implementation with LLMs as Assessors

Expert annotation such as the one we perform in Section [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2#S5 "5. TTCW Implementation with Experts as Assessors ‣ Art or Artifice? Large Language Models and the False Promise of Creativity") is costly: based on our evaluation protocol, evaluating a 1500-2500 word short story with a qualified expert costs $20, and requires roughly 30 minutes of the expert’s time. Prior work has shown the promise of using LLMs in text evaluation. For instance, GPT3.5 and GPT4 are effective at evaluating the factual consistency of a summary to its document ([Laban et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2#bib.bib41)), or measuring the coherence of a summary ([Gao et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2#bib.bib29)). GPTEval ([Liu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2#bib.bib43)) employs the framework of using large language models with chain-of-thoughts (CoT) ([Wei et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2#bib.bib71)) to assess the quality of NLG outputs. Recent work has also applied LLM-based evaluation to the creative domain ([Rajani et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2#bib.bib57)), claiming that GPT4 can achieve a high correlation with humans when evaluating brainstorming or creative generation tasks. In this section, we describe our TTCW implementation with LLMs as assessors to understand LLM’s ability to assess creative writing.

We apply a similar evaluation protocol to the one described in Section [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2#S5 "5. TTCW Implementation with Experts as Assessors ‣ Art or Artifice? Large Language Models and the False Promise of Creativity"). We use the same data selection and the same three LLMs: GPT3.5, GPT4, and Claude, and prompt them to answer the 14 individual TTCW tests for the 48 stories in our collection.

Prior work [Wei et al. (2022)](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2#bib.bib71) has shown how generating a chain of thought – a series of intermediate reasoning steps – significantly improves the ability of large language models to perform complex reasoning. Taking advantage of this we design the prompts/instructions for large language models in a slightly different fashion than for the human experts as can be seen in Table [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2.1. Expanded Expert Measure and Prompt Design ‣ 5.2. Evaluation Protocol ‣ 5. TTCW Implementation with Experts as Assessors ‣ Art or Artifice? Large Language Models and the False Promise of Creativity")(Row 3 (Human Instruction) vs Row 4 LLM Instruction). To help the model make an informed decision we first ask it to list out elements specific to any given test such as “elements in the story that call to each of the five senses" for the World Building and setting test followed by asking it to decide overall and then provide its reasoning before choosing an answer between ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. The exact prompt contains (1) the story, (2) the expanded TTCW context, (3) the TTCW question, and (4) an LLM-specific instruction guiding the model to perform the task in a chain-of-though manner. We then measure model agreement with the majority vote of the three experts that conducted the test, using Cohen’s Kappa.
