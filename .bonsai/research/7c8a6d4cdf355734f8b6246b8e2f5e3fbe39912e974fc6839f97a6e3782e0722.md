## 6 Formulation of Verbosity Bias

In the second experiment, we observed the tendency of LLMs to have low human alignment for cases where human feedback preferred shorter answers. In this section, we formulate verbosity bias to allow for quantative comparison between models.

In our problem setting, we define the given pair of text inputs as y0y\_{0} and y1y\_{1}, the LLM outputs decision as Y′∈{0,1}Y^{\\prime}\\in\\{0,1\\}, and the more helpful option labeled by humans as Y∈{0,1}Y\\in\\{0,1\\}. We define the sensitive attribute S∈{0,1}S\\in\\{0,1\\} which equals 0 when y0y\_{0} has more words than y1y\_{1}, and 1 when y1y\_{1} has more words than y0y\_{0}.

With these definitions, equal opportunity ([Hardt et al.,, 2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.10076#bib.bib5)) with respect to sensitive attribute SS is satisfied if

|  | P⁡(Y′=0\|S=0,Y=0)=P⁡(Y′=0\|S=1,Y=0).\displaystyle P(Y^{\prime}=0\|S=0,Y=0)=P(Y^{\prime}=0\|S=1,Y=0). |  | (3) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

This only accounts for cases where human feedback prefers y0y\_{0}. Although this can be attained by sorting the inputs beforehand, the equation can be generalized with accuracy parity instead of equal opportunity. Accuracy parity is satisfied if the accuracy of prediction is equal among both demographics:

|  | P⁡(Y′=Y\|S=Y)=P⁡(Y′=Y\|S=1−Y).\displaystyle P(Y^{\prime}=Y\|S=Y)=P(Y^{\prime}=Y\|S=1-Y). |  | (4) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

The deviance from accuracy parity can be calculated with the following equation:

|  | \|P⁡(Y′=Y\|S=Y)−P⁡(Y′=Y\|S=1−Y)\|.\displaystyle\|P(Y^{\prime}=Y\|S=Y)-P(Y^{\prime}=Y\|S=1-Y)\|. |  | (5) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

Even though this is how the deviance is calculated in general, we thought it important that the directional information of the bias isn’t lost. With the formulation below ([6](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.10076#S6.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 6 Formulation of Verbosity Bias ‣ Verbosity Bias in Preference Labeling by Large Language Models")), a positive value indicates that the LLM prefers verbose answers, and a negative value indicates it prefers shorter answers. This distinction is crucial as some tasks may have a negative bias, for example in summarization tasks as shown in [Huang et al., (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.10076#bib.bib6). We also opted for the difference in inaccuracy between demographics

|  | P⁡(Y′=1−Y\|S=1−Y)−P⁡(Y′=1−Y\|S=Y)\displaystyle P(Y^{\prime}=1-Y\|S=1-Y)-P(Y^{\prime}=1-Y\|S=Y) |  | (6) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

because verbosity bias refers to the inacuracy influenced by verbosity.

[Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.10076#S6.T1 "In 6 Formulation of Verbosity Bias ‣ Verbosity Bias in Preference Labeling by Large Language Models") shows the verbosity bias values of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 calculated with data from [Section 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.10076#S5 "5 Is There A Difference in Verbosity Preference Between LLMs and Humans? ‣ Verbosity Bias in Preference Labeling by Large Language Models"). From these numbers, we can conclude that GPT-4 has improved in verbosity bias. Compared to [Wang et al., (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.10076#bib.bib11), which had a limited problem setting and gave the impression that GPT-4 is significantly less prone to verbosity bias, we see that the verbosity bias still exists for GPT-4. A further experiment on other LLMs for comparison is required.

| --- | --- | --- |
