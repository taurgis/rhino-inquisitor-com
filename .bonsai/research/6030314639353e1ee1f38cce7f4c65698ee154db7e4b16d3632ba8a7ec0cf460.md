### 5.1. Cultural Differences in AI Attitudes

As we showed in Section [4.1], Indian participants exhibited higher engagement with AI suggestions: a larger proportion of their essays were composed of AI-generated text, and they accepted more AI suggestions compared to Americans. However, we argue that this is not a flaw, but an important finding of our study. To fully understand the differential AI engagement seen in our study, we turn to HCI scholarship for a socio-technical view of human-AI interaction.

The way people perceive the benefits and harms of emerging technologies varies across cultures and geographic regions ([Liu et al., 2024]). For instance, people in individualistic cultures (e.g., the US) tend to evaluate new technologies through direct and formal sources, while those in collectivistic cultures (e.g., India) rely more on peer feedback ([Lee et al., 2013]). AI technologies are no different—they are cultural artifacts ([Dourish, 2016]) and perceptions about them vary across cultures, both at the national ([Eitle and Buxmann, 2020]) and user levels ([Liu et al., 2024]). Research shows that users in non-Western settings express more positive emotions toward conversational agents, whereas users in the US tend to be more ambivalent ([Liu et al., 2024]). Non-Western users also show higher levels of trust in AI technologies than their American counterparts ([Shin et al., 2022]). This positive attitude often manifests as overreliance on AI ([Buçinca et al., 2021]), especially among novice users in non-Western settings ([Okolo et al., 2024]).

These cultural differences in AI overreliance may be exacerbated as embedded AI applications reach more diverse users. For instance, in writing applications, presenting explanations for every suggestion may disrupt the user’s flow, hindering productivity. Furthermore, our study shows that embedded AI applications promising productivity gains may inadvertently push non-Western users toward overreliance by delivering diminished benefits to them.

We argue that higher trust and AI reliance are cultural characteristics that significantly influence human-AI interaction. It is important to acknowledge these cross-cultural differences in trust, adoption, and degree of AI reliance.

## Detailed

### 5.1. Cultural Differences in AI Attitudes

As we showed in Section [4.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#S4.SS1 "4.1. Does AI impact Indians and Americans differently? ‣ 4. Findings ‣ AI Suggestions Homogenize Writing Toward Western Styles and Diminish Cultural Nuances"), Indian participants exhibited higher engagement with AI suggestions: a larger proportion of their essays were composed of AI-generated text, and they accepted more AI suggestions compared to Americans. From a purely causal lens, this difference in AI engagement could be seen as a confounding factor, potentially undermining our findings. For example, it could suggest that the homogenization effects are simply a result of Indians’ higher AI usage. However, we argue that this is not a flaw, but an important finding of our study. To fully understand the differential AI engagement seen in our study, we turn to HCI scholarship for a socio-technical view of human-AI interaction.

The way people perceive the benefits and harms of emerging technologies varies across cultures and geographic regions ([Liu et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib54)). For instance, people in individualistic cultures (e.g., the US) tend to evaluate new technologies through direct and formal sources, while those in collectivistic cultures (e.g., India) rely more on peer feedback ([Lee et al., 2013](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib52)). AI technologies are no different—they are cultural artifacts ([Dourish, 2016](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib23)) and perceptions about them vary across cultures, both at the national ([Eitle and Buxmann, 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib24)) and user levels ([Liu et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib54)). Research shows that users in non-Western settings express more positive emotions toward conversational agents, whereas users in the US tend to be more ambivalent ([Liu et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib54)). Non-Western users also show higher levels of trust in AI technologies than their American counterparts ([Shin et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib79)). In writing contexts, particularly when composing in English, AI-based writing suggestions provide more utility to non-native speakers than to native speakers, leading them to engage more with the suggestions ([Buschek et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib14)). This positive attitude often manifests as overreliance on AI ([Buçinca et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib13)), especially among novice users in non-Western settings ([Okolo et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib63)).

These cultural differences in AI overreliance may be exacerbated as embedded AI applications reach more diverse users. While past research has shown that overreliance can be mitigated with AI explanations ([Vasconcelos et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib85); [Solano-Kamaiko et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11360#bib.bib81)), this approach becomes less viable in new-age interactive and embedded AI tools, where providing explanations may not be feasible. For instance, in writing applications, presenting explanations for every suggestion may disrupt the user’s flow, hindering productivity. Furthermore, our study shows that embedded AI applications promising productivity gains may inadvertently push non-Western users toward overreliance by delivering diminished benefits to them.

We argue that higher trust and AI reliance are cultural characteristics that significantly influence human-AI interaction. It is important to acknowledge these cross-cultural differences in trust, adoption, and degree of AI reliance. By trying to eliminate them in our analyses as mere confounders, we may fail to notice the cultural harms they are causing, risking a future where cultural nuances are erased.
