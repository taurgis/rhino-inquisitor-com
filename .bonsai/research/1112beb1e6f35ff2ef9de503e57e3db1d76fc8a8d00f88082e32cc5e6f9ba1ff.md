## 1 Introduction

Self-evaluation is becoming a prominent part of the large language model (LLM) lifecycle. LLM evaluators are shown to be highly accurate at approximating human annotators on various tasks, and are significantly more scalable ([Hackl et al., 2023]).

In self-evaluation, as the name suggests, the same underlying LLM acts as both the evaluator and the evaluatee. As a result, the neutrality of the evaluator is in question, and the evaluation can suffer from biases where the LLM evaluators diverge from humans in systematic ways ([Zheng et al., 2024]; [Bai et al., 2024]). One such bias is self-preference, where an LLM rates its own outputs higher than texts written by other LLMs or humans, while human annotators judge them as equal quality.

Towards understanding and mitigating self-preference, we study self-recognition—an LLM’s capability of recognizing its own outputs. We ask: Is self-preference truly self\-preference, in the sense that the LLM prefers a text because it was generated by itself?

We measure their correlation while using prompting and fine-tuning to alter the LLM’s self-recognition capability. In order to provide signals for the causal link between self-recognition and self-preference, we also fine-tune the LLM on a comprehensive set of potential confounding properties.

Our main findings are as follows:

1.  1.
    
    Frontier LLMs exhibit self-preference in self-evaluation. On two summarization tasks, LLMs (GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4, and Llama 2) disproportionately favor summaries written by themselves over those by other LLMs and from humans.

3.  3.
    
    Fine-tuning leads to near-perfect self-recognition. GPT-3.5 and Llama 2 both achieve over 90%90\\% accuracy at self-recognition after fine-tuning on 500 examples.

## 1 Introduction

Self-evaluation is becoming a prominent part of the large language model (LLM) lifecycle. In methods like reward modeling ([Leike et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib20); [Stiennon et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib36)), model-based benchmarks ([Shashidhar et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib33); [Zeng et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib45); [Yuan et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib44); [Fu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib10); [Li et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib21)), self-refinement ([Saunders et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib32); [Madaan et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib23); [Lee et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib19); [Shridhar et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib34)), and constitutional AI ([Bai et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib2)), LLMs are increasingly used to provide assessment, supervision, and oversight for themselves and other LLMs. LLM evaluators are shown to be highly accurate at approximating human annotators on various tasks, and are significantly more scalable ([Hackl et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib11)).

In self-evaluation, as the name suggests, the same underlying LLM acts as both the evaluator and the evaluatee. One such bias is self-preference, where an LLM rates its own outputs higher than texts written by other LLMs or humans, while human annotators judge them as equal quality. Self-preference has been observed in GPT-4-based dialogue benchmarks ([Bitton et al., 2023b](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib6); [Koo et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib16)), as well as for text summarization ([Liu et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076#bib.bib22)).
