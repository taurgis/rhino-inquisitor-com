## 2 Background and Related Work

#### AI-associated lexical shifts in scientific writing.

Scientific English is undergoing a notable lexical shift, in which words such as _delve_, _underscore_, and _intricate_ have spiked since 2022 [Liang et al. (2024b)]; [Liang et al. (2024a)]; [Liu and Bu (2024)]; [Matsui (2024)]; [Masukume (2024)]; [Picazo-Sanchez and Ortiz-Martin (2024)]; [Juzek and Ward (2025)]; [Kobak et al. (2025)]; [Kousha and Thelwall (2025)]; [Geng et al. (2026)]; [Botes et al. (2025)]. This overuse departs from historical baselines [Kobak et al. (2025)]; [Liang et al. (2025b)], and many of the same words are overused in AI-generated text [Juzek and Ward (2025)]. Furthermore, the analysis of AI-associated shifts connects to the broader literature on diachronic lexical semantic change, which examines how word meanings evolve over time [Hamilton et al. (2016)]; [Tahmasebi et al. (2018)]; [Schlechtweg et al. (2020)]. Existing analyses of AI-associated shifts (which concern prevalence rather than meaning) have, however, often relied on manually selected or filtered lists, which makes it harder to extend them across languages.

#### Beyond scientific writing.

Evidence suggests that lexical shifts are not confined to academic writing. Since ChatGPT’s release, shifts towards AI-preferred words have also been observed in spoken communication, including unscripted spoken English [Yakura et al. (2024)]; [Anderson et al. (2025)]. This raises the possibility of homogenisation pressures on content diversity [Moon et al. (2025)]; [Padmakumar and He (2023)]; [Doshi and Hauser (2024)]; [Zhang et al. (2025a)], and of effects on creativity [Anderson et al. (2024)]; [Kumar et al. (2025)]; [Wenger and Kenett (2025)] and on cross-cultural expression [Agarwal et al. (2025)]; [Sourati et al. (2025)]; [Utami and Ryohei (2026)]. LLM-generated content has also been detected at scale in English news [Hanley and Durumeric (2024)], Italian news [Puccetti et al. (2024)], and broader English web ecosystems [Sun et al. (2025)], as well as reported in professional workplace writing [Liebscher et al. (2026)].

#### Mechanisms.

State-of-the-art chat assistants typically undergo several training stages: pretraining, instruction tuning, preference learning, and task-specific fine-tuning.

Recent work shows that preference-based alignment can introduce systematic biases, including preference collapse [Xiao et al. (2024)], verbosity bias [Saito et al. (2023)]; [Wu and Aji (2025)], sycophancy [Sharma et al. (2023)]; [Wei et al. (2023)], and reduced output diversity [Kirk et al. (2023)]; [Murthy et al. (2025)]; [Zhang et al. (2025b)]; [Zhang et al. (2025a)]. More broadly, alignment may reward stylistic properties that are socially legible as helpful, safe, or sophisticated, and thereby reinforce a distinctive assistant register characterised by hedging, positivity, and recurrent lexical choices [Gabriel (2020)]; [Durmus et al. (2023)]; [Santurkar et al. (2023)]; [He et al. (2024)]; [Norhashim and Hahn (2024)]; [Young et al. (2024)]; [Bharadwaj et al. (2025)]; [Juzek and Ward (2025)]; [Chooi (2026)]; [Huang et al. (2026)].

#### Beyond scientific writing.

Evidence suggests that lexical shifts are not confined to academic writing. Since ChatGPT’s release, shifts towards AI-preferred words have also been observed in spoken communication, including unscripted spoken English [Yakura et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib121); [Anderson et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib4). More broadly, AI tools may accelerate latent trends towards specific stylistic norms [Rudnicka (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib92); [Rudnicka (2025)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib93), and algorithmic suggestions can steer writing towards more predictable forms [Arnold et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib5); [Hohenstein et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib39). This raises the possibility of homogenisation pressures on content diversity [Moon et al. #### Mechanisms.

State-of-the-art chat assistants typically undergo several training stages: pretraining, instruction tuning, preference learning, and task-specific fine-tuning. Whereas pretraining provides broad statistical knowledge of language and instruction tuning improves assistant-like responsiveness, preference-learning methods such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback and related optimisation frameworks further steer outputs towards responses preferred by human raters [Christiano et al. (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib21); [Ziegler et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib129); [Stiennon et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib107); [Ouyang et al. (2022)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib84); [Bai et al. (2022a)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib7); [Bai et al. (2022b)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib8); [Rafailov et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib89). However, this stage optimises not only for correctness or usefulness, but also for properties that human annotators tend to reward.

Recent work shows that preference-based alignment can introduce systematic biases, including preference collapse [Xiao et al. (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib120), verbosity bias [Saito et al. (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib52); [Murthy et al. (2025)](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25358v1#bib.bib76); [Zhang et al.
