## Detailed

### 2.1 Excess words indicate widespread LLM usage

We downloaded all PubMed abstracts until the end of 2024 and used all 15.1 M English-language abstracts from 2010 onwards (after cleaning them from contaminating strings, see Methods). We then computed the matrix of word occurrences that shows which abstracts contain which words, resulting in a 15.1 M ×\\times 273 K sparse binary matrix. For each word and each year, we found the number of abstracts in that year that the word appeared in, and obtained its occurrence frequency pp by normalizing with the total number of papers published in that year. Our main analysis focused on 26.7 K words with p\>10−4p>10^{-4} in both 2023 and 2024. With over 1 million abstracts per year, this corresponds to \>100{>}100 usages per year.

Some words strongly increased their occurrence frequency in 2023–2024 (Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary")). To quantify this increase, we calculated counterfactual expected frequency in 2024 based on the linear extrapolation of word frequencies in 2021 and 2022 (see Methods). Note that we did not use 2023 frequencies for calculating the expected frequency, because they could already have been affected by LLM usage. Comparing the empirical 2024 frequency pp with the expected 2024 frequency qq, we obtained the excess frequency gap δ\=p−q\\delta=p-q and the excess frequency ratio r\=p/qr=p/q as two measures of excess usage. These two measures are complementary. The frequency gap is well-suited to highlight excess usage of frequent words, while the frequency ratio points to the excess usage of infrequent words. For example, frequency increases from 0.001 to 0.01 and from 0.5 to 0.6 are both noteworthy. Yet, the former frequency increase is captured by a high rr value whereas the latter has a high δ\\delta value.

Across all 26.7 K words, we found many with strong excess usage in 2024 (Figure [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary")). Less common words with strong excess usage included delves (r\=28.0r=28.0), underscores (r\=13.8r=13.8), and showcasing (r\=10.7r=10.7), together with their grammatical inflections (Figure [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary")a). More common words with strong excess usage included potential (δ\=0.052\\delta=0.052), findings (δ\=0.041\\delta=0.041), and crucial (δ\=0.037\\delta=0.037) (Figure [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary")b).

Is this unusual, or do similar frequency changes happen every year? For comparison, we did the same analysis for all years from 2013 to 2023 (Figures [S1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#Sx3.F1 "Figure S1 ‣ Supplementary Figures ‣ Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary")–[S4](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#Sx3.F4 "Figure S4 ‣ Supplementary Figures ‣ Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary")). We found words like ebola with r\=9.9r=9.9 in 2015 and zika with r\=40.4r=40.4 in 2017, but from 2013 until 2019, no single word has ever shown excess frequency gap δ\>0.01\\delta>0.01. This changed during the Covid pandemic: in 2020–2022 words like coronavirus, covid, lockdown, and pandemic showed very large excess usages (up to r\>1000r>1000 and δ\=0.06\\delta=0.06), in agreement with the observation that the Covid pandemic had an unprecedented effect on biomedical publishing ([González-Márquez et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07016v5#bib.bib20)).
